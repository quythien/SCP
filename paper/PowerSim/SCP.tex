\title{\textsc{SCP}: Simulation-Based Power Calculation and Study Design for Circadian Rhythmic Analysis}
\author{}
\date{}

\begin{document}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Circadian transcriptomic studies are often limited by sample availability, uncontrolled collection times, and genome-wide multiple testing, yet existing power tools do not support pilot-calibrated planning for circadian biomarker detection and differential circadian analysis. We present \textsc{SCP}, a simulation-based framework for sample-size planning in circadian transcriptomics. \textsc{SCP} uses pilot-derived gene-level amplitude, noise, phase, and time-of-day distributions to estimate FDR-controlled power for single-cohort circadian biomarker detection and two-group differential analyses of rhythmicity, phase, and MESOR. It also quantifies pilot-driven uncertainty by bootstrap and extends the single-harmonic cosinor test to a two-harmonic F-test for detecting non-sinusoidal rhythmic biomarkers. Across public human pilots, differences in the pilot effect-size distribution led to large differences in the sample size required to achieve target power. For balanced equally spaced active designs above the harmonic identifiability threshold, power is primarily governed by total sample size rather than the split between sampling times and replicates.

\textbf{Availability and implementation:} \textsc{SCP} is implemented in R and available at \url{https://github.com/quythien/SCP}. Documentation and example data are provided with the repository.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Circadian rhythms, endogenous $\sim$24-hour oscillations, play a fundamental role in physiology and disease \citep{takahashi2017transcriptional}. Disruption of circadian timing has been implicated in aging and neuropsychiatric disorders \citep{chen2016aging}, and rhythmic transcription regulates metabolism, synaptic function, and immune pathways \citep{takahashi2017transcriptional,Mure2018baboon,chen2016aging}. Circadian transcriptomic studies usually pursue one of two aims: detecting rhythmic biomarkers within a condition or characterizing cross-condition changes in rhythmic patterns such as differential rhythmicity (DR), differential phase (DP), and differential MESOR (rhythm-adjusted mean expression; DM). Methods such as \textsc{JTK\_CYCLE}, \textsc{RAIN}, and \textsc{BIO\_CYCLE} support single-condition rhythm detection \citep{Hughes2010jtkcycle,Thaben2014rain,Agostinelli2016biocycle}. \textsc{CircaCompare}, \textsc{LimoRhyde}, and \textsc{DiffCircaPipeline} support cross-condition analysis \citep{parsons2020circacompare,Singer2019LimoRhyde,xue2023diffcircapipeline}. These tools address inference after data collection; they do not tell investigators how large a study must be to detect the signals of interest.

Prospective study design therefore remains a critical bottleneck. Investigators must choose a sample size, evaluate how much the recommendation depends on the pilot dataset, and, in active experimental designs, decide how to allocate samples across time points and biological replicates. Passive observational settings, such as human post-mortem studies, are especially difficult because collection time cannot be controlled and each additional sample is costly. Without design guidance calibrated to the planned tissue, assay, and collection-time distribution, circadian transcriptomic studies can remain underpowered despite substantial experimental investment.

To our knowledge, \textsc{CircaPower} \citep{zong2023circapower} is currently the only dedicated framework for power and sample-size determination in circadian transcriptomic studies, but it is restricted to single-condition rhythmicity detection. CircaPower derives closed-form power under a cosinor model using a fixed intrinsic effect size \( r = A/\sigma \). In practice, $r$ varies substantially across tissues, species, and genes, with marked differences between core clock genes and the broader transcriptome \citep{zong2023circapower}. A fixed-$r$ calculation therefore cannot represent transcriptome-wide signal heterogeneity or the false discovery rate (FDR) behavior induced by testing thousands of genes.

Differential circadian analysis adds another layer of complexity. DR, DP, and DM are distinct biological endpoints, and workflows such as \textsc{DiffCircaPipeline} first classify joint rhythmicity status before applying endpoint-specific tests \citep{xue2023diffcircapipeline}. Power and FDR then depend on data-derived strata, effect-size thresholds, and genome-wide multiplicity. This multi-stage structure is not reducible to a single closed-form test statistic.

These gaps motivate \textbf{\textsc{SCP}} (Simulation-based Circadian Power), a pilot-calibrated simulation framework for circadian transcriptomic study design. \textsc{SCP} estimates transcriptome-wide power under BH FDR control for single-cohort rhythmicity detection and extends the same simulation logic to DR, DP, and DM in two-group studies. It also propagates pilot-estimation uncertainty through an outer bootstrap and provides a two-harmonic F-test that generalizes the single-harmonic cosinor test while preserving a linear nested-model form. The Results evaluate these components on public human circadian transcriptomic pilots and balanced active-design simulations.

\subsection{Power calculation for circadian biomarker detection in a single cohort}
\label{sec:single_cohort}

For a single cohort of $N$ subjects with sample collection time-of-day (TOD)
$t_1,\ldots,t_N\in[0,24)$, the expression of gene $g$ follows the cosinor
model \citep{cornelissen2014cosinor}
\begin{equation}
y_{gi}
=
\mu_g
+
A_g\cos \bigl(\omega_0 (t_i - \phi_g)\bigr)
+
\varepsilon_{gi},
\qquad
\varepsilon_{gi}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}N(0,\sigma_g^2),
\label{eq:cosinor_model}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_0 = 2\pi/24$ is the angular frequency of the $24$-hour cycle, $\mu_g$ is the MESOR (midline estimating statistic of rhythm; i.e., rhythm-adjusted mean expression), $A_g\geq 0$ is the amplitude,
$\phi_g\in[0,24)$ is the acrophase, and $\sigma_g$ is the noise level. \citet{zong2023circapower} established the theoretical foundations of cosinor-based power calculation under Gaussian errors. To test rhythmicity for gene $g$, we use the $F$-statistic for $H_0: A_g = 0$ versus $H_A: A_g > 0$:
\begin{equation}
F_g
=
\frac{(\mathrm{TSS}_g - \mathrm{RSS}_g)/2}{\mathrm{RSS}_g/(N-3)}
\sim F(2, N-3),
\label{eq:rhythm_f_test}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{RSS}_g$ and $\mathrm{TSS}_g$ are the residual and total sums of
squares from the cosinor fit and $F(a,\, b)$ is an F-distribution with degrees of freedom $a$ and $b$. Across $G$ genes, Benjamini--Hochberg (BH)
correction \citep{benjamini1995controlling} at nominal FDR level $\alpha$
controls FDR for rhythmicity detection.

For power calculation, under the alternative hypothesis $H_A$, the $F$-statistic follows a noncentral $F$-distribution:
$F_g\sim  F\bigl( 2, N-3, \lambda_g\bigr)$ with degrees of freedom $2$ and $N-3$ and noncentrality parameter $\lambda_g$. The per-gene statistical power at sample size $N$ is then
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Power}_g(N)
=
1 - F\bigl(q_{\alpha}; 2, N-3, \lambda_g\bigr),
\label{eq:circapower_formula}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $q_{\alpha} = F^{-1}(1 - \alpha;\, 2, N - 3)$ is the null rejection threshold at level $\alpha$, $F(\cdot;\, a, b)$ denotes the CDF of a central $F(a, b)$, and $F(\cdot;\, a, b, \lambda)$ denotes the CDF of a noncentral $F(a, b, \lambda)$.

\citet{zong2023circapower} showed that
\begin{equation}
\lambda_g = r_g^2 \cdot N \cdot d(\phi_g),
\label{eq:ncp}
\end{equation}
where $r_g = A_g/\sigma_g$ is the intrinsic effect size of gene $g$, and the design factor $d(\phi_g)$ captures how well the sample collection times $(t_1,\cdots,t_N)$ cover the 24-hour cycle:
\begin{equation}
d(\phi_g)
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \cos^2\bigl(\omega_0 (t_i - \phi_g)\bigr)
-
\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \cos\bigl(\omega_0 (t_i - \phi_g)\bigr)\right)^{\!2}.
\label{eq:design_factor_circapower}
\end{equation}

For balanced equally spaced active designs with $B\geq 3$ time points,
$d(\phi_g)=1/2$ for all phases, so \eqref{eq:ncp} reduces to
$\lambda_g = r_g^2 N / 2$ and power depends only on $r_g$ and $N$. This
$B$-invariance is consistent with the design-factor result of
\textsc{CircaPower} \citep{zong2023circapower}; see
Section~\ref{sec:active_passive}.

In transcriptome-wide studies, however, $r_g$ varies substantially across
genes: core clock genes in mouse liver typically have $r \approx 3$--$6$,
while the median $r$ across all expressed genes in human brain tissue is
$\tilde{r}\approx 0.6$ \citep{zong2023circapower}.


A single representative $r$, as in \textsc{CircaPower}, can therefore produce
over-optimistic power projections for much of the transcriptome,
and the per-gene formula \eqref{eq:circapower_formula} does not account for the
multiple-testing correction applied when testing thousands of genes simultaneously.
\textsc{SCP} addresses these limitations through the following four-step
simulation pipeline.



\emph{Step 1: Pilot parameter estimation.}
For each of the $G_0$ pilot genes, fit \eqref{eq:cosinor_model} by OLS to obtain
$(\hat{\mu}_g, \hat{A}_g, \hat{\sigma}_g, \hat{\phi}_g)$ and the corresponding
$p$-value $p_g$ for rhythmicity. To improve stability across datasets with
heterogeneous signal strength, we define a pilot-stage screening rule based on
a fixed marginal threshold $\alpha_{\text{pilot}}$ (default $\alpha_{\text{pilot}}=0.01$),
rather than using multiplicity-adjusted thresholds. Let
$\mathcal{G}_R^{\mathrm{cand}} = \{g : p_g < \alpha_{\text{pilot}}\}$ and
$K_{\mathrm{top}} = \min\bigl(300,\ |\mathcal{G}_R^{\mathrm{cand}}|\bigr)$, and
define the parameter-estimation set as the top-ranked genes
$\mathcal{G}_R = \mathrm{Top}_{K_{\mathrm{top}}}(\mathcal{G}_R^{\mathrm{cand}})$.
The estimated rhythmic proportion is given by
$\hat{\pi}_R = |\mathcal{G}_R^{\mathrm{cand}}| / G_0$.
Empirical distributions are then constructed over the fitted parameters:
\begin{equation}
\hat{F}_\mu
=
\frac{1}{G_0}\sum_{g=1}^{G_0}\delta_{\hat{\mu}_g},
\quad
\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}_R|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_R}\delta_{(\hat{A}_g,\hat{\sigma}_g)},
\quad
\hat{F}_{\phi}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}_R|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_R}\delta_{\hat{\phi}_g}.
\label{eq:empirical_dists}
\end{equation}
The mesor distribution $\hat{F}_\mu$ is estimated from all $G_0$ genes and reflects a
shared baseline-expression level across the transcriptome regardless of rhythmic status.
We assume a shared empirical mesor distribution across genes, while allowing
gene-specific mesor shifts between groups under the DM state
(Section~\ref{sec:differential}).
Sampling $(A,\sigma)$ jointly from $\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$ preserves the empirical
amplitude-noise dependence, so the induced
$r=A/\sigma$ distribution reflects this relationship without requiring $r$ to be specified separately.
The complete pilot summary $\hat{\Psi} = (\hat{F}_\mu, \hat{F}_{A,\sigma},
\hat{F}_\phi, \hat{F}_{\mathrm{TOD}}, \hat{\pi}_R)$ is passed to the downstream
steps, where $\hat{F}_{\mathrm{TOD}}$ is the empirical collection-time
distribution for passive designs. The choice of $\alpha_{\text{pilot}}$ can be
adjusted by the user. In practice an adequate pilot shares tissue,
condition, and assay platform with the planned study and meets the
identifiability threshold for the intended detector ($B \geq 3$ for
the single-harmonic cosinor F-test, $B \geq 5$ for the two-harmonic F-test). The pilot summary
$\hat{\Psi}$ is itself a sample-based estimator: at small
$n_{\mathrm{pilot}}$, residual variability in $\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$ and
$\hat{\pi}_R$ propagates to non-trivial uncertainty in the projected
power curve, which Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap} quantifies through a
nonparametric bootstrap.

\emph{Step 2: Data generation.}
For each simulation replicate $s=1,\ldots,S$ and candidate sample size $N$,
draw rhythmicity indicators $Z_{g,s}^R \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\hat{\pi}_R)$
independently for $g=1,\ldots,G$. Conditional on $Z_{g,s}^R$, observations are
generated from a two-component mixture model
\[
y_{g,i}^{(s)} \mid Z_{g,s}^R \sim
\begin{cases}
M_1\!\left(\mu_g^{(s)}, A_g^{(s)}, \sigma_g^{(s)}, \phi_g^{(s)}\right), & Z_{g,s}^R = 1, \\[6pt]
M_0\!\left(\mu_g^{(s)}, \sigma_g^{(s)}\right), & Z_{g,s}^R = 0,
\end{cases}
\]
where the rhythmic model $M_1$ is the cosinor model
\[
M_1:\quad
y_{g,i}^{(s)} = \mu_g^{(s)} + A_g^{(s)} \cos\!\bigl(\omega_0 (t_i - \phi_g^{(s)})\bigr)
+ \varepsilon_{g,i}^{(s)}, \qquad
\varepsilon_{g,i}^{(s)} \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(0, (\sigma_g^{(s)})^2\bigr),
\]
and the arrhythmic model $M_0$ is the reduced intercept-only model
\[
M_0:\quad
y_{g,i}^{(s)} = \mu_g^{(s)} + \varepsilon_{g,i}^{(s)}, \qquad
\varepsilon_{g,i}^{(s)} \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(0, (\sigma_g^{(s)})^2\bigr).
\]

For genes with $Z_{g,s}^R=1$, parameters are sampled as
$\mu_g^{(s)}\sim\hat{F}_\mu$, $(A_g^{(s)},\sigma_g^{(s)})\sim\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$,
and $\phi_g^{(s)}\sim\hat{F}_\phi$. For genes with $Z_{g,s}^R=0$, we set
$A_g^{(s)}=0$, sample $\mu_g^{(s)}\sim\hat{F}_\mu$, and sample $\sigma_g^{(s)}$
from the empirical marginal distribution of $\sigma$ implied by $\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$.

An \emph{active} design specifies $B$ distinct time points with $m_b$ replicates
each and total $N=\sum_b m_b$; a \emph{passive} design draws collection times
from $\hat{F}_{\mathrm{TOD}}$. For balanced equally spaced active designs with
$B\geq 3$, $d(\phi)=1/2$ for all phases, so power depends only on total $N$;
for passive designs the realized $d(\phi_g)$ is computed from the observed
collection times via \eqref{eq:design_factor_circapower}, varies by gene and
replicate, and \textsc{SCP} reports design-factor diagnostics. 




\emph{Step 3: Inference.}
Apply the $F$-test \eqref{eq:rhythm_f_test} to every gene and BH correction at
nominal level $\alpha$ across all $G$ genes. Let $D_{g,s}\in\{0,1\}$ denote
the discovery indicator ($D_{g,s}=1$ if gene $g$ is declared rhythmic in
replicate $s$).

\emph{Step 4: Power aggregation.}
With $Z_{g,s}^R\in\{0,1\}$ the true rhythmicity status assigned in Step~2,
empirical power is
\[
\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}(N)
=
\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^S
\frac{\sum_g D_{g,s}Z_{g,s}^R}{\sum_g Z_{g,s}^R},
\]
and empirical FDR is the per-replicate fraction of false discoveries among all
discoveries, averaged across $S$ replicates. Because parameters are drawn from
the full pilot distributions $\hat{F}_\mu$, $\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$, and $\hat{F}_\phi$
(Step~1), the power estimate integrates over the observed transcriptome-wide
heterogeneity in baseline expression, amplitude, noise, and phase rather than
relying on a single representative effect size. Because BH
correction is applied within each replicate (Step~3), genome-wide multiplicity
control is captured naturally rather than treated as a post-hoc adjustment.
The indicators $D_{g,s}$ and $Z_{g,s}^R$ introduced here carry over directly
to Section~\ref{sec:differential}, where endpoint-specific superscripts
distinguish among differential endpoints.

A computationally efficient alternative to the full simulation pipeline is also available for the single-cohort setting. Equation~\eqref{eq:circapower_formula}
is evaluated gene-by-gene at each pilot gene's estimated
$\hat{r}_g = \hat{A}_g/\hat{\sigma}_g$ and design factor
$\hat{d}(\hat{\phi}_g)$, and the results are averaged to yield
$\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}_{\mathrm{CF}}(N)$.
This approximation is computationally efficient, but it does not propagate
genome-wide FDR or the joint $(A_g,\sigma_g)$ heterogeneity. The full
simulation-based pipeline is therefore recommended when transcriptome-wide
operating characteristics are the primary design target.

\subsection{Power calculation for case-control differential circadian analysis}
\label{sec:differential}

The two-group setting evaluates three biological endpoints: a gene
gains or loses 24-hour rhythmicity between conditions (DR), keeps
rhythmicity but shifts its peak in time (DP), or maintains its
rhythm shape but shifts its mean expression level (DM). Each
endpoint is tested separately within a TOJR-based two-stage
procedure, so the single-group indicators $D_{g,s}$ and $Z_{g,s}^R$
from Section~\ref{sec:single_cohort} generalize to endpoint-indexed
quantities. For each endpoint
$E\in\{\mathrm{DR},\mathrm{DP},\mathrm{DM}\}$,
$D_{g,s}^E\in\{0,1\}$ denotes the endpoint-specific discovery indicator and
$Z_{g,s}^E\in\{0,1\}$ denotes the true differential status. A targeted indicator $Z_{g,s}^{*,E} = Z_{g,s}^E \cdot \mathbf{1}\{\delta_{g,s}^E \geq \Delta_E\}$ restricts attention to genes whose effect size exceeds a user-specified threshold $\Delta_E$, where
$\delta_{g,s}^{\mathrm{DR}} = |R_{g,1,s}^2 - R_{g,2,s}^2|$ is the absolute difference in cosinor goodness-of-fit between groups,
$\delta_{g,s}^{\mathrm{DP}} = \min\!\bigl(|\phi_{g,1,s}-\phi_{g,2,s}|,\,24-|\phi_{g,1,s}-\phi_{g,2,s}|\bigr)$ is the circular phase distance,
and $\delta_{g,s}^{\mathrm{DM}} = |\mu_{g,2,s} - \mu_{g,1,s}|$ is the absolute mesor difference.
Here $R_{g,k,s}^2$ for $k \in \{1, 2\}$ denotes the coefficient of
determination of the cosinor model fit restricted to group $k$, that
is, $R_{g,k,s}^2 = 1 - \mathrm{SSE}_{g,k,s}/\mathrm{SST}_{g,k,s}$,
where $\mathrm{SSE}$ and $\mathrm{SST}$ are the residual and total sums
of squares of the group-$k$ cosinor fit. The group index $k$ used here
is distinct from the harmonic index $k$ in
Section~\ref{sec:cosinor_violation}.

When comparing rhythmic patterns between two groups, inference becomes
multi-stage: genes are first partitioned by per-group rhythmicity, followed by
endpoint-specific tests within data-derived strata. Because the multi-stage inference procedure does not yield tractable analytical
expressions for power and FDR, these quantities are evaluated via simulation
following the full analysis pipeline.

\textsc{SCP} evaluates three endpoints: differential rhythmicity (DR), differential
phase (DP), and differential MESOR (DM). DR captures gain or loss of rhythmicity
(e.g., clock genes that cycle in healthy tissue but lose rhythmicity in disease);
DP captures a shift in peak-expression timing; and DM captures a change in
baseline expression level while rhythmic structure is preserved.
Their simulation proportions are denoted
$(\pi_{\mathrm{DR}}, \pi_R, \pi_{\mathrm{DP}}, \pi_{\mathrm{DM}})$.
When pilot data for both groups are available, $\pi_{\mathrm{DR}}$ and
$\pi_{\mathrm{DP}}$ are estimated empirically from the two-group pilot comparison;
$\pi_{\mathrm{DM}}$ is always user-specified, as mesor differences are not reliably
identifiable from the pilot distributions used to calibrate baseline parameters.
Otherwise all proportions are user-specified, with $\pi_R$ initialized from
$\hat{\pi}_R$ by default. When group-specific pilot data are available, the shared
summary $\hat{\Psi}$ can be replaced by group-specific summaries
$\hat{\Psi}_1$ and $\hat{\Psi}_2$, each estimated from the corresponding pilot,
to capture group-level heterogeneity in baseline parameters.
The simulation follows the same four-step structure as
Section~\ref{sec:single_cohort}, with Step~1 shared.

\emph{Step 2: Two-group data generation.}
For each gene, a baseline parameter tuple
$(\mu_g^{(s)}, A_g^{(s)}, \sigma_g^{(s)}, \phi_g^{(s)})$
is sampled from the pilot summary
$\hat{\Psi}=(\hat{F}_{\mu},\hat{F}_{A,\sigma},\hat{F}_{\phi},\hat{F}_{\mathrm{TOD}})$
constructed in Step~1, with $\hat{F}_\mu$ estimated from all $G_0$ genes and
$\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$, $\hat{F}_\phi$ from the rhythmic set $\mathcal{G}_R$.
A latent type $T_g \in \{0,1,2,3,4,5\}$ is then independently assigned
by sampling from a categorical distribution with type probabilities
\[
P(T_g = 1) = \pi_R, \quad
P(T_g = 2) = P(T_g = 3) = \pi_{\mathrm{DR}} / 2, \quad
P(T_g = 4) = \pi_{\mathrm{DP}}, \quad
P(T_g = 5) = \pi_{\mathrm{DM}},
\]
and
$P(T_g = 0) = 1 - \pi_R - \pi_{\mathrm{DR}} - \pi_{\mathrm{DP}} -
\pi_{\mathrm{DM}}$,
where differential rhythmicity is split equally between loss
($T_g = 2$, rhythmic in group 1 only) and gain ($T_g = 3$, rhythmic in
group 2 only).

Group-specific parameters are initialized by setting
$\mu_{g,1}^{(s)} = \mu_{g,2}^{(s)} = \mu_g^{(s)}$,
$A_{g,1}^{(s)} = A_{g,2}^{(s)} = A_g^{(s)}$, and
$\phi_{g,1}^{(s)} = \phi_{g,2}^{(s)} = \phi_g^{(s)}$, and are then modified
according to $T_g$. Observations are generated as
\[
y_{g,i,k}^{(s)} \sim M_{T_g}(\mu_{g,k}^{(s)}, A_{g,k}^{(s)}, \sigma_g^{(s)}, \phi_{g,k}^{(s)}),
\quad k\in\{1,2\},
\]
where $M_1$ denotes the cosinor model and $M_0$ the intercept-only model,
with Gaussian noise $\varepsilon_{g,i,k}^{(s)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,(\sigma_g^{(s)})^2)$.

Specifically, $T_g=0$ yields arrhythmic genes with $A_{g,1}=A_{g,2}=0$ and
$y_{g,i,k}^{(s)}=\mu_g^{(s)}+\varepsilon_{g,i,k}^{(s)}$ (model $M_0$),
whereas $T_g=1$ yields jointly rhythmic genes with shared parameters across
groups following $M_1$. For differential rhythmicity, $T_g=2$ sets $A_{g,2}=0$
and $T_g=3$ sets $A_{g,1}=0$, so that one group follows $M_1$ and the other
$M_0$. For differential phase ($T_g=4$), both groups follow $M_1$ with
$\phi_{g,2} = (\phi_{g,1} + \Delta \phi_g)\bmod 24$ and
$\Delta \phi_g \sim \mathrm{Unif}(\Delta \phi_{\min}, \Delta \phi_{\max})$;
for differential MESOR ($T_g=5$), both groups follow $M_1$ with
$\mu_{g,2} = \mu_{g,1} + \Delta \mu_g$ and
$\Delta \mu_g \sim \mathrm{Unif}(\Delta \mu_{\min}, \Delta \mu_{\max})$.

\emph{Step 3: DiffCircaPipeline inference.}
Inference proceeds via \textsc{DiffCircaPipeline}. For each group $k$,
cosinor models are fit and $p$-values $p_{g,k}$ are obtained. Genes are
assigned Test-of-Joint-Rhythmicity (TOJR) labels
\citep{xue2023diffcircapipeline}:
\[
\hat{Z}_g =
\begin{cases}
\texttt{arrhy}   & p_{g,1}>\alpha \text{ and } p_{g,2}>\alpha,\\
\texttt{g1-only} & p_{g,1}\leq\alpha \text{ and } p_{g,2}>\alpha,\\
\texttt{g2-only} & p_{g,1}>\alpha \text{ and } p_{g,2}\leq\alpha,\\
\texttt{both}    & p_{g,1}\leq\alpha \text{ and } p_{g,2}\leq\alpha.
\end{cases}
\]
where $\alpha$ denotes the inference-stage threshold, distinct from the
pilot-stage threshold $\alpha_{\text{pilot}}$ used in Step~1. Endpoint-specific
tests are then applied within strata, followed by BH correction at level $\alpha$.

\emph{Step 4: Power and FDR summarization.}
For each endpoint $E$, let $R_s^E=\sum_g D_{g,s}^E$ and
$V_s^E=\sum_g D_{g,s}^E(1-Z_{g,s}^E)$. The per-replicate FDR is
$\widehat{\mathrm{FDR}}_s^E = V_s^E/R_s^E$ when $R_s^E>0$, and 0 otherwise.
Targeted power is
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{TPower}}_s^E(\Delta_E)
=
\frac{\sum_g D_{g,s}^E Z_{g,s}^{*,E}}{\sum_g Z_{g,s}^{*,E}},
\label{eq:tpower_def}
\end{equation}
where the dependence on $\Delta_E$ enters through the targeted
indicator $Z_{g,s}^{*,E} = Z_{g,s}^E \cdot \mathbf{1}\{\delta_{g,s}^E
\geq \Delta_E\}$ defined above. Final summaries are obtained by
averaging across simulation replicates.





\subsection{Bootstrap uncertainty in projected power}
\label{sec:bootstrap}

The procedure in Section~\ref{sec:single_cohort} is plug-in: the pilot summary
$\hat{\Psi}$ is estimated once and treated as fixed when computing the projected
power curve $\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}(N)$. When the pilot cohort is small,
however, the estimated amplitude--noise distribution
$\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$ and rhythmic proportion $\hat{\pi}_R$ may vary
substantially across pilot samples. This variation creates uncertainty in the
resulting design recommendation.

To quantify this uncertainty, \textsc{SCP} uses an outer nonparametric
bootstrap over pilot subjects \citep{efron1979bootstrap}. We describe the
procedure in the single-cohort setting for concreteness; the differential
setting in Section~\ref{sec:differential} uses the same outer resampling and
inner simulation structure, applied to endpoint-specific targeted power curves
rather than $\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}(N)$. For each bootstrap replicate
$b = 1, \ldots, B_{\mathrm{out}}$: \emph{(i)} resample subjects with replacement
from the pilot cohort; \emph{(ii)} recompute the pilot summary
$\hat{\Psi}^{(b)}$; and \emph{(iii)} run the full inner simulation pipeline to
obtain $\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}^{(b)}(N)$.

The bootstrap mean
$\bar{\mathrm{Power}}(N)
=
B_{\mathrm{out}}^{-1}
\sum_{b=1}^{B_{\mathrm{out}}}
\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}^{(b)}(N)$,
together with the empirical 2.5\% and 97.5\% quantiles of
$\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}^{(b)}(N)$, provides a bootstrap confidence band
reflecting uncertainty in the pilot-derived empirical distributions and
rhythmic proportion contained in $\hat{\Psi}$. The procedure is nested:
outer resampling captures pilot-to-pilot variability, whereas inner simulations
capture gene-level Monte Carlo variability conditional on the resampled pilot.

The bootstrap should therefore be interpreted as quantifying uncertainty in the
design recommendation rather than correcting finite-sample bias. In very small
or weakly rhythmic pilots, both the plug-in estimate and the bootstrap mean may
remain unstable because the pilot-derived empirical distributions are themselves
estimated with limited information. In larger, well-powered pilots
($n_{\mathrm{pilot}} \gtrsim 250$), the plug-in curve is typically stable and
the bootstrap band mainly serves as a sensitivity diagnostic.

\subsection{A two-harmonic cosinor extension}
\label{sec:cosinor_violation}

The single-harmonic model in \eqref{eq:cosinor_model} captures only the
fundamental 24-hour component. However, circadian transcriptomic profiles
can also contain substantial 12-hour structure
\citep{hughes2009harmonics,zhu201712hour}. To accommodate this setting,
we extend the cosinor model to include a second harmonic:
\begin{equation}
y_{gi}
=
\mu_g
+
A_{g,1}\cos \bigl(\omega_0 (t_i - \phi_{g,1})\bigr)
+
A_{g,2}\cos \bigl(2\omega_0 (t_i - \phi_{g,2})\bigr)
+
\varepsilon_{gi},
\qquad
\varepsilon_{gi}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}N(0,\sigma_g^2).
\label{eq:twoharm_model}
\end{equation}
The first harmonic represents the 24-hour component, while the second
harmonic represents the 12-hour component. Setting $A_{g,2}=0$ recovers
the single-harmonic model in \eqref{eq:cosinor_model}.

Estimation is performed using the equivalent linear parameterisation
\begin{equation}
y_{gi}
=
\mu_g
+
a_{g,1}\cos(\omega_0 t_i)
+
b_{g,1}\sin(\omega_0 t_i)
+
a_{g,2}\cos(2\omega_0 t_i)
+
b_{g,2}\sin(2\omega_0 t_i)
+
\varepsilon_{gi}.
\label{eq:twoharm_linear}
\end{equation}
For $k\in\{1,2\}$,
$
A_{g,k}=\sqrt{a_{g,k}^2+b_{g,k}^2}
$,
so the harmonic amplitudes are recovered directly from the linear
coefficients. This linear representation avoids phase non-identifiability
under the null hypothesis of no rhythmicity.

For the $K=2$ detector used in the main examples, rhythmicity is tested by
the joint null hypothesis
\[
H_0:
a_{g,1}=b_{g,1}=a_{g,2}=b_{g,2}=0,
\]
equivalently $A_{g,1}=A_{g,2}=0$. Relative to the single-harmonic test in
\eqref{eq:rhythm_f_test}, the numerator degrees of freedom increase from
$2$ to $4$:
\begin{equation}
F_g
=
\frac{(\mathrm{TSS}_g - \mathrm{RSS}_g)/4}
{\mathrm{RSS}_g/(N-5)}
\sim F(4,\,N-5)
\qquad \text{under } H_0 .
\label{eq:twoharm_f_test}
\end{equation}
BH correction is then applied across genes as in
Section~\ref{sec:single_cohort}. Thus, the $K=2$ detector tests the joint
contribution of the 24-hour and 12-hour harmonic components.

For $K=2$, the regression design contains five columns: the intercept and
four harmonic basis terms,
$\cos(\omega_0 t)$, $\sin(\omega_0 t)$, $\cos(2\omega_0 t)$, and
$\sin(2\omega_0 t)$. Because repeated samples at the same phase increase
precision but do not add new design rows, full column rank requires at
least five distinct sampling phases per period, giving the active-design
condition $B \geq 5$. Thus, the $K=2$ extension captures 12-hour harmonic
structure while retaining a parsimonious and identifiable regression
model. This design requirement follows directly from the regression basis
and is consistent with genome-scale circadian design guidance recommending
dense temporal sampling when subcircadian or ultradian structure is of
interest \citep{Hughes2017guidelines}. Higher-order harmonic regression
follows analogously by adding paired cosine and sine terms for
$k=1,\ldots,K$, but is outside the scope of the current manuscript and
software release.

\subsection{Pilot datasets and simulation setup}
\label{sec:pilot_datasets}

Across all figures, simulations were calibrated from public human circadian transcriptomic pilots: postmortem \textsc{GTEx} \citep{gtex2020atlas} tissues and the Ketchesin human striatum cohort \citep{ketchesin2021diurnal} (GSE160521). Passive-design analyses sampled collection times from each pilot's empirical time-of-day distribution; the balanced active-design analysis reused the pilot effect-size distribution under a planned equally spaced sampling grid. We summarised pilot signal strength by $\tilde{r}$, the median gene-level effect size $r_g = A_g/\sigma_g$ over the top-$K_{\mathrm{top}}$ rhythmic genes used to calibrate $\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$ in Step 1 (Section~\ref{sec:single_cohort}), with $K_{\mathrm{top}} = \min(300, |\mathcal{G}_R|)$ and $\mathcal{G}_R$ the genes passing the cosinor pre-screen at $\alpha_{\mathrm{pilot}} = 0.01$. Differential power in Figure~\ref{fig:diff_adrliv} used the \textsc{GTEx} Adrenal Gland versus Liver comparison. The two-harmonic analyses in Figures~\ref{fig:twoharm_demo} and~\ref{fig:twoharm_power} used \textsc{GTEx} Liver. The balanced active-design comparison in Figure~\ref{fig:active_vs_passive} used the Ketchesin Putamen control cohort (GSE160521; $n_{\text{pilot}} = 59$, $\tilde{r} \approx 1.06$). The bootstrap analysis in Figure~\ref{fig:bootstrap_sc} contrasted Ketchesin Putamen schizophrenia controls (GSE160521; $n_{\text{pilot}} = 28$) and \textsc{GTEx} Thyroid ($n_{\text{pilot}} = 416$). All simulations used Benjamini-Hochberg FDR control at $\alpha = 0.05$; simulation replicate counts are given in the captions where relevant.

Single-cohort power is driven by the $\tilde{r}$ distribution rather than any

simulation, rather than a single effect size, is essential for realistic study
planning.

\subsection{Bootstrap uncertainty: pilot size and plug-in bias}
\label{sec:bootstrap_results}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/Fig3_bootstrap_singlecohort.pdf}
\caption{Bootstrap uncertainty in single-cohort power estimates,
contrasting the smallest and largest pilots in our test set. Blue
curve: plug-in power (parameters estimated once from pilot, treated
as fixed). Red curve with error bars: bootstrap mean power and
pointwise $95\%$ CI ($B_{\mathrm{out}} = 50$ outer resamples,
$N_{\mathrm{sim}}^{\mathrm{in}} = 25$ inner simulations, single-harmonic cosinor detector at the FDR threshold
$0.05$). Both pilots are passive. \textbf{(a)} Putamen schizophrenia
controls (GSE160521; $n_{\mathrm{pilot}} = 28$): plug-in projects
$28\%$ power at $N = 40$ while the bootstrap mean is $43\%$, and the
$95\%$ bootstrap CI at $N = 40$ is $[33.5\%, 54.0\%]$.
\textbf{(b)} GTEx Thyroid ($n_{\mathrm{pilot}} = 416$): widest $95\%$
CI at $N = 120$ is $[51.4\%, 57.8\%]$ and the plug-in mean tracks the
bootstrap mean within $\pm 3.5\%$ across the full grid. The
contrast shows that bootstrap uncertainty assessment matters most when
the pilot is small, and that pilot-induced variability saturates against the
inner-simulation noise floor for large pilots.}
\label{fig:bootstrap_sc}
\end{figure}

Two features of Figure~\ref{fig:bootstrap_sc} carry the bootstrap
message. First, the plug-in and bootstrap mean diverge most strongly
at the small pilot: at Putamen-SCZ $N = 40$, plug-in is $27.7\%$ but
the bootstrap mean is $42.6\%$, a $15$-point gap that the plug-in
curve alone hides. The bootstrap CI at the same $N$ spans $33.5\%$ to
$54.0\%$, so even averaging across $50$ resamples does not collapse
the uncertainty in the recommendation. Second, for the largest pilot
(\textsc{GTEx} Thyroid, $n_{\mathrm{pilot}} = 416$) the $95\%$ band
tightens to within $\pm 3.5\%$ of the plug-in estimate at the elbow
of the curve, dominated by inner-simulation Monte Carlo rather than
pilot variability; further increases in $n_{\mathrm{pilot}}$ would
not materially tighten the recommendation. The bootstrap is therefore
most useful for $n_{\mathrm{pilot}} \lesssim 40$ and often optional for
$n_{\mathrm{pilot}} \gtrsim 250$ at this inner-simulation budget; the
intermediate-pilot behaviour interpolates monotonically between these
two regimes. Because the uncertainty comes from the pilot summary
$\hat{\Psi}$, the same bootstrap logic applies to two-harmonic analyses.

\subsection{Two-harmonic testing improves recovery of non-sinusoidal rhythms}
\label{sec:twoharm_results}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/Fig4_twoharm_demo.pdf}
\caption{Two-harmonic rhythmicity detection on the \textsc{GTEx} Liver
pilot ($8917$ genes, BH-FDR $0.05$). \textbf{(A)} Single-harmonic
(dashed) and two-harmonic ($K=2$; solid) fits for four exemplar genes,
annotated with the genome-wide BH $q$-values from each $F$-test
($q_{1H}$, $q_{2H}$); \textit{ARNTL} is near-sinusoidal and detected by
both, while \textit{RORC}, \textit{IK}, and \textit{GBA} carry
second-harmonic structure and cross $0.05$ only under $K = 2$.
\textbf{(B)} The single-harmonic test detects $210$ genes ($3$ unique)
and the two-harmonic test $725$ ($518$ unique), with $207$ shared.
\textbf{(C)} The two-harmonic-only set is led by lysosome biogenesis
($35$ genes, adjusted $p \approx 3.9\times 10^{-6}$).}
\label{fig:twoharm_demo}
\end{figure}

On the \textsc{GTEx} Liver pilot, the second harmonic substantially broadened rhythmic gene recovery at BH-FDR $0.05$ (Figure~\ref{fig:twoharm_demo}). Across $8917$ genes, the single-harmonic $F$-test in \eqref{eq:rhythm_f_test} declared $210$ genes rhythmic, whereas the two-harmonic test in \eqref{eq:twoharm_f_test} declared $725$. Most of the additional yield came from genes seen only by the richer model: the overlap was $207$, with $518$ genes recovered only by the two-harmonic test and just $3$ in the reverse direction (Figure~\ref{fig:twoharm_demo}B). This pattern is expected when within-day structure includes appreciable 12-hour signal, because the second harmonic contributes to the combined effect size $r_{g,\mathrm{eff}}^2 = r_{g,1}^2 + r_{g,2}^2$ (with $r_{g,k} = A_{g,k}/\sigma_g$), which is inaccessible to the fundamental-only test.

The gene-level fits show that this is not a marginal numerical effect but a change in which waveforms become detectable (Figure~\ref{fig:twoharm_demo}A). \textit{ARNTL} behaves as an approximately sinusoidal transcript, and both tests identify it cleanly, with $q_{1H} \approx 1.5\times 10^{-9}$ and $q_{2H} \approx 7.3\times 10^{-11}$. The more informative examples are \textit{RORC}, \textit{IK}, and \textit{GBA}. For \textit{RORC}, the single-harmonic result is $q_{1H} \approx 0.091$, but the two-harmonic fit reaches $q_{2H} \approx 0.024$. For \textit{IK}, the corresponding values are $q_{1H} \approx 0.30$ and $q_{2H} \approx 0.035$. For \textit{GBA}, they are $q_{1H} \approx 0.14$ and $q_{2H} \approx 0.002$. In each case, detection at $0.05$ requires $K = 2$, consistent with visible second-harmonic structure in the fitted profile.

The added discoveries also appear biologically organized rather than indiscriminate. The two-harmonic-only set was led by KEGG enrichment for lysosome biogenesis, with $35$ genes and adjusted $p \approx 3.9\times 10^{-6}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:twoharm_demo}C). That coherence is consistent with structured non-sinusoidal signal in the additional calls.

\label{sec:twoharm_power}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/Fig5_twoharm_framework.pdf}
\caption{Operating characteristics of the two-harmonic ($K=2$) $F$-test on the
\textsc{GTEx} Liver pilot ($N_{\mathrm{sim}} = 100$). \textbf{(A)} Marginal power increases
monotonically in $N$ and in the FDR target; at BH-FDR $0.05$ the
interpolated $N_{80}$ is approximately $250$ on this low-effect-size
pilot. \textbf{(B)} Power stratified by $r_{\mathrm{eff}}$ at BH-FDR
$0.05$; curves shift upward with $N$ and saturate above
$r_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 0.75$, with negligible power in the lowest
stratum.}
\label{fig:twoharm_power}
\end{figure}

The corresponding simulation shows that this gain still depends on the pilot effect-size distribution (Figure~\ref{fig:twoharm_power}). Marginal power increased monotonically in $N$ and in the FDR target. At $N = 200$, power was approximately $48\%$ at $\alpha = 0.01$, $66\%$ at $\alpha = 0.05$, $74\%$ at $\alpha = 0.10$, and $82\%$ at $\alpha = 0.20$; at BH-FDR $0.05$, the interpolated $N_{80}$ was approximately $250$. The natural interpretation is not that the detector is inefficient, but that \textsc{GTEx} Liver has modest combined rhythmic amplitudes: detection power increases with $r_{g,\mathrm{eff}}$, so a small $r_{g,\mathrm{eff}}$ distribution necessarily pushes $N_{80}$ upward.

Effect-size stratification makes that dependence explicit (Figure~\ref{fig:twoharm_power}B). At fixed $N$, power increased monotonically across $r_{\mathrm{eff}}$ strata and approached $100\%$ once $r_{\mathrm{eff}} \gtrsim 0.75$. At the opposite extreme, the lowest stratum, $r_{\mathrm{eff}} < 0.25$, remained near zero even at $N = 200$. This ordering reflects the monotone dependence of power on $r_{g,\mathrm{eff}}$: the strata expected to be hardest are precisely the ones that stay underpowered across the simulation grid. As in the single-cohort setting (Figure~\ref{fig:sc_adrliv}), the wide spread in power within one dataset underscores why projections must be anchored to the full pilot-derived effect-size distribution rather than to a single summary.

\subsection{Active-design power is governed by total sample size above identifiability}
\label{sec:active_passive}

\begin{figure}[!htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/Fig6_active_BvsM.pdf}
\caption{Power under balanced active sampling on the Ketchesin Putamen
control pilot (GSE160521; $n_{\mathrm{pilot}} = 59$,
$\tilde{r}\approx 1.06$; $N_{\mathrm{sim}} = 100$, BH-FDR
$0.05$). \textbf{(A)} Single-harmonic power is approximately invariant
in $B$ at fixed $N$ (curves within ${\sim}1\%$ across $B$) and reaches
$80\%$ near $N = 50$. \textbf{(B)} Two-harmonic power above the
identifiability threshold $B \geq 5$ is likewise approximately
$B$-invariant (within ${\sim}1.5\%$ across $B \in \{6, 8, 12, 24\}$) and
also reaches $80\%$ near $N = 50$; at small $N$ it sits modestly below
the single harmonic on this near-cosinor pilot.}
\label{fig:active_vs_passive}
\end{figure}

Under balanced active sampling on the Ketchesin Putamen control pilot, power was effectively determined by total sample size once the design cleared the identifiability bound, not by how samples were partitioned across time points (Figure~\ref{fig:active_vs_passive}). For the single-harmonic test, where $B \geq 3$, the six grids $B \in \{3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24\}$ overlapped within about $1\%$ at every $N$, and each reached $80\%$ power near $N = 50$. The same pattern held for the two-harmonic test after identifiability, with $B \geq 5$: the admissible grids $B \in \{6, 8, 12, 24\}$ overlapped within about $1.5\%$ and also reached $80\%$ power near $N = 50$, while $B \in \{3, 4\}$ were rank-deficient and undefined. This mirrors the single-harmonic design invariance of Section~\ref{sec:single_cohort}: under a balanced equally spaced design with $B$ above $2K + 1$, power depends on $N$ and the combined effect size $r_{g,\mathrm{eff}}$ rather than on the allocation $(B, m)$.

The cost of fitting additional harmonic degrees of freedom was visible on this near-cosinor pilot. At $N = 16$, the achieved power was approximately $35\%$ for $K = 1$ and $27\%$ for $K = 2$. At $N = 20$, it was approximately $46\%$ for $K = 1$ and $40\%$ for $K = 2$. That gap closed by $N \approx 30$, after which the two curves coincided within sampling error. The reason is straightforward: in \eqref{eq:twoharm_f_test}, the second-harmonic test uses two additional numerator degrees of freedom, and on this pilot those extra parameters return little noncentrality because 12-hour structure is weak. Once the identifiability bound is met, $B \geq 3$ for $K = 1$ and $B \geq 5$ for $K = 2$, the choice between $B$ and $m$ reduces to the choice of total $N$; detector performance is then driven by waveform shape, not by the sampling grid or by the active-versus-passive distinction itself.

\section{Discussion}
%% ====================================================================

\textsc{SCP} addresses a practical gap in circadian transcriptomic study
design: power depends on the full pilot-derived signal distribution, not
on a nominal effect size. In the evaluated human pilots, changing only the
pilot tissue was enough to move the required single-cohort sample size by
more than a factor of four. These results underscore the need for
pilot-calibrated power analysis that preserves the empirical distributions
of amplitude, noise, rhythmic phase, and time-of-day sampling, while also
respecting the genome-wide FDR structure of the downstream analysis.


Differential circadian endpoints are harder to plan analytically because
inference depends on rhythmicity classification before endpoint-specific
testing. The simulation framework makes that dependence explicit: DR, DP,
and DM can have different sample-size requirements even under the same
pilot comparison, and all require more information than single-cohort
rhythmicity detection. This is where simulation is most useful, because it
preserves the two-stage structure of \textsc{DiffCircaPipeline} rather
than replacing it with a simplified one-test approximation.

The bootstrap component provides a diagnostic for the stability of
pilot-based design recommendations. When the pilot is small, uncertainty
in $\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$ and $\hat{\pi}_R$ can be large enough to change a
design recommendation; when the pilot is large, the plug-in power curve is
usually sufficient. Thus the bootstrap should be reported when the
confidence band would affect the planned sample size, not treated as a
mandatory correction for every analysis.

The two-harmonic extension adds a second design choice: the detector
should match the waveform. The single-harmonic cosinor test is efficient
for near-sinusoidal rhythms, whereas the two-harmonic test can recover
structured 12-hour signal that the fundamental-only test misses. The price
is the expected degrees-of-freedom penalty when the added harmonic
contributes little signal, especially at small $N$. This trade-off argues
for using $K=2$ when pilot profiles or prior biology support
non-sinusoidal structure and the planned sampling design satisfies the
identifiability threshold.

For balanced equally spaced active designs, the allocation question
simplifies once the identifiability threshold is met. Above
$B \geq 2K+1$, the harmonic-regression design has sufficient temporal
resolution to estimate the corresponding sine and cosine basis terms. This
does not mean that all active and passive designs are equivalent: passive
designs can still pay an efficiency cost when collection times are uneven.
Rather, within balanced active designs, investigators should first ensure
temporal identifiability and then plan around total sample size.

Taken together, these results provide practical guidance for prospective
circadian transcriptomic study design. Detector choice should be guided
primarily by waveform shape: the single-harmonic cosinor test remains a
parsimonious option for near-sinusoidal rhythms, whereas the two-harmonic
$F$-test is better suited when pilot profiles or prior biology suggest
substantial 12-hour structure and the planned design satisfies $B \geq 5$.
Once the detector is chosen, the sampling grid should clear the
corresponding identifiability threshold, with $B \geq 3$ for the
single-harmonic test and $B \geq 5$ for the two-harmonic test. Above this
threshold, balanced active designs are driven primarily by total sample
size rather than by the specific $(B,m)$ allocation. Sample-size targets
can then be read from the pilot-calibrated power curve, using the
single-cohort pipeline for rhythmic biomarker detection and the
differential pipeline for DR, DP, and DM. Bootstrap confidence bands are
most useful when pilot uncertainty is large enough to affect the final
design recommendation.

Several limitations apply. The current framework is built around
parametric cosinor and two-harmonic regression models; it does not yet
provide calibrated power calculations for nonparametric rhythmicity
detectors such as rank- or shape-based tests. The single- and
two-harmonic $F$-tests assume Gaussian residuals and may lose calibration
for heavily sharp-peaked, near-arrhythmic, or heteroskedastic genes. The
differential simulator treats endpoint types as mutually exclusive, so
compound rhythmicity-plus-phase changes are not directly evaluated.
Passive-design power depends on the pilot's empirical collection-time
distribution, which may not generalize to a planned cohort with different
recruitment logistics. The bootstrap is computationally intensive and is
recommended when pilot uncertainty is likely to affect the design
decision.
