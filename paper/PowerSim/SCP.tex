\begin{abstract}
Circadian disruption is a hallmark of numerous human diseases, yet detecting condition-specific changes in gene expression rhythms remains a significant challenge. Circadian transcriptomic studies aim to identify rhythmic biomarkers within a given condition or to characterize differential circadian biomarkers across conditions. Given the constraints of limited budgets and sample availability, rigorous power calculation and thoughtful study design are critical for planning such studies. Existing approaches, such as CircaPower, offer closed-form analytical solutions for circadian biomarker detection but do not account for gene-specific signal-to-noise ratios or genome-wide false discovery. To address these limitations, we present SCP (Simulation-based Circadian Power), a simulation-based framework for the study design of circadian transcriptomic analyses. Using gene-specific information from pilot or publicly available data, SCP enables pilot-calibrated, FDR-controlled power calculations across the transcriptome. The framework supports sample size determination for circadian biomarker detection and accommodates multi-faceted differential rhythm analyses, including differential rhythmicity, differential phase, and differential MESOR. For active experimental designs such as in animal studies, we further investigate the trade-off between sampling frequency and biological replicates and resolve it analytically: above the harmonic-regression identifiability threshold, power depends on total sample size alone. For pilots whose waveforms deviate from a pure cosinor, SCP also provides a K-harmonic F-test that generalises the cosinor test to user-tunable harmonic order, stacking $K$ periodic bases and testing the joint harmonic null with a nested F-statistic. Additionally, a bootstrap procedure is introduced to quantify uncertainty in power curves and sample size recommendations. SCP is evaluated on simulated and real circadian datasets across active and passive design regimes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Circadian rhythms, endogenous $\sim$24-hour oscillations, play a fundamental role in physiology and disease \citep{takahashi2017transcriptional}. Disruption of circadian timing has been implicated in aging and neuropsychiatric disorders \citep{chen2016aging}, and rhythmic transcription regulates metabolism, synaptic function, and immune signaling \citep{takahashi2017transcriptional,mure2018diurnal,chen2016aging}. To unveil circadian regulation in transcriptomic data, studies generally pursue one of two goals: detecting potential circadian biomarkers within a single condition or characterizing differences in rhythmic patterns across conditions, including differential rhythmicity (DR), differential phase (DP), and differential MESOR (i.e., rhythm-adjusted mean expression) (DM). A growing number of methods have been developed for both settings, including rhythmicity detection methods such as \textsc{JTK\_CYCLE}, \textsc{RAIN}, and \textsc{BIO\_CYCLE} for single-condition studies \citep{Hughes2010jtkcycle,Thaben2014rain,Agostinelli2016biocycle}, and differential frameworks such as \textsc{CircaCompare}, \textsc{LimoRhyde}, and \textsc{DiffCircaPipeline} for cross-condition analysis \citep{parsons2020circacompare,Singer2019LimoRhyde,xue2023diffcircapipeline}. While these methods enable statistical inference on circadian signals, they do not address how studies should be designed to achieve reliable detection.

Prospective study design therefore remains a critical bottleneck. Investigators must determine the sample size required to achieve adequate statistical power, assess how sensitive those recommendations are to the limitations of pilot data, and, in active experimental designs, strike the right balance between sampling frequency and biological replicates. In passive observational settings, such as human post-mortem studies, sampling time cannot be controlled and sample acquisition is costly, making principled sample size calculation especially important. Without rigorous design guidance, circadian transcriptomic studies risk being substantially underpowered despite considerable experimental investment.

To our knowledge, \textsc{CircaPower} \citep{zong2023circapower} is currently the only dedicated framework for power and sample size determination in circadian transcriptomic studies, but it is restricted to single-condition rhythmicity detection and does not extend to differential analyses. CircaPower derives closed-form power under a cosinor model using a fixed intrinsic effect size \( r = A/\sigma \). In practice, $r$ varies substantially across tissues, species, and genes, with marked differences between core clock genes and the broader transcriptome \citep{zong2023circapower}, so a fixed-$r$ assumption misrepresents transcriptome-wide signal strength. Because circadian studies operate at the transcriptome-wide scale, false discovery rate (FDR) control is also required, and the fixed-$r$ formulation provides no mechanism to model the cross-gene variability needed to characterize genome-wide error rates.

For power calculation of differential circadian biomarker detection, closed-form analytical solutions analogous to those in CircaPower are generally infeasible. As established in DiffCircaPipeline \citep{xue2023diffcircapipeline}, circadian rhythm disruptions are complex and multi-faceted, encompassing differential rhythmicity (DR), differential phase (DP), and differential MESOR (DM). Detection of these features requires a two-stage workflow contingent on the rhythmicity status inferred for each condition. From a statistical standpoint, this biological complexity involves multiple endpoints coupled with genome-wide multiple testing, and therefore cannot be reduced to a single test statistic or a tractable closed-form solution.

These gaps motivate the development of \textbf{SCP} (Simulation-based Circadian Power), a simulation-based framework for power calculation and study design that supports practical decision-making in planning circadian transcriptomic studies. The framework is organised in four methodological components. Section~\ref{sec:single_cohort} develops per-gene power for circadian biomarker detection in a single cohort, with parameter distributions calibrated to pilot data and BH control of the genome-wide false discovery rate. Section~\ref{sec:differential} extends the construction to multi-faceted differential rhythm analysis in a two-group setting, covering differential rhythmicity (DR), differential phase (DP), and differential MESOR (DM). Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap} introduces an outer bootstrap that propagates pilot-estimation uncertainty into the projected power curves, a source of variability that plug-in power calculations omit. Section~\ref{sec:cosinor_violation} extends the cosinor model to a two-harmonic form that captures non-sinusoidal, 12-hour ultradian structure while remaining a nested $F$-test. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports simulations and real-data illustrations, including the active-versus-passive design comparison (Section~\ref{sec:active_passive}), that benchmark the framework end-to-end.

\subsection{Power calculation for circadian biomarker detection in a single cohort}
\label{sec:single_cohort}

For a single cohort of $N$ subjects with sample collection time-of-day (TOD)
$t_1,\ldots,t_N\in[0,24)$, the expression of gene $g$ follows the cosinor
model \citep{cornelissen2014cosinor}
\begin{equation}
y_{gi}
=
\mu_g
+
A_g\cos \bigl(\omega_0 (t_i - \phi_g)\bigr)
+
\varepsilon_{gi},
\qquad
\varepsilon_{gi}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}N(0,\sigma_g^2),
\label{eq:cosinor_model}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_0 = 2\pi/24$ is the angular frequency of the $24$-hour cycle, $\mu_g$ is the MESOR (Midline Estimating Statistic Of Rhythm; i.e., rhythm-adjusted mean expression), $A_g\geq 0$ is the amplitude,
$\phi_g\in[0,24)$ is the acrophase, and $\sigma_g$ is the noise level. \citet{zong2023circapower} established the theoretical foundations of cosinor-based power calculation under Gaussian errors. To test rhythmicity for gene $g$, we use the $F$-statistic for $H_0: A_g = 0$ versus $H_A: A_g > 0$:
\begin{equation}
F_g
=
\frac{(\mathrm{TSS}_g - \mathrm{RSS}_g)/2}{\mathrm{RSS}_g/(N-3)}
\sim F(2,, N-3),
\label{eq:rhythm_f_test}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{RSS}_g$ and $\mathrm{TSS}_g$ are the residual and total sums of
squares from the cosinor fit and $F(a,\, b)$ is F-distribution with degrees of freedom $a$ and $b$. Across $G$ genes, Benjamini--Hochberg (BH)
correction \citep{benjamini1995controlling} at nominal FDR level $\alpha$
controls FDR for rhythmicity detection.

For power calculation, under the alternative hypothesis $H_A$, the $F$-statistic follows a noncentral $F$-distribution:
$F_g\sim  F\bigl( 2,, N-3, \, \lambda_g\bigr)$ with degrees of freedom $2$ and $N-3$ and noncentrality parameter $\lambda_g$. The per-gene statistical power at sample size $N$ is then
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Power}_g(n)
=
1 - F\bigl(q_{\alpha}; 2,, N-3, \, \lambda_g\bigr),
\label{eq:circapower_formula}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $q_{\alpha} = F^{-1}(1 - \alpha;, 2,, N - 3)$ is the null rejection threshold at level $\alpha$, $F(\cdot;\, a, b)$ denotes the CDF of a central $F(a, b)$, and $F(\cdot;\, a, b, \lambda)$ denotes the CDF of a noncentral $F(a, b, \lambda)$.

\citet{zong2023circapower} showed that
\begin{equation}
\lambda_g = r_g^2 \cdot N \cdot d(\phi_g),
\label{eq:ncp}
\end{equation}
where $r_g = A_g/\sigma_g$ is the intrinsic effect size of gene $g$, and the design factor $d(\phi_g)$ captures how well the sample collection TOD $(t_1,\cdots, t_n)$ spread on time domain:
\begin{equation}
d(\phi_g)
=
\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \cos^2\bigl(\omega_0 (t_i - \phi_g)\bigr)
-
\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^N \cos\bigl(\omega_0 (t_i - \phi_g)\bigr)\right)^{\!2}.
\label{eq:design_factor_circapower}
\end{equation}

% Brief B-invariance statement with a forward pointer; full derivation
% is reserved for Section~\ref{sec:active_passive} and Supplementary~S6.
For balanced equally spaced active designs with $B\geq 3$ time points,
$d(\phi_g)=1/2$ for all phases, so \eqref{eq:ncp} reduces to
$\lambda_g = r_g^2 N / 2$ and power depends only on $r_g$ and $N$; see
Section~\ref{sec:active_passive} and Supplementary~S6.

In transcriptome-wide studies, however, $r_g$ varies substantially across
genes: core clock genes in mouse liver typically have $r \approx 3$--$6$,
while the median $r$ across all expressed genes in human brain tissue is
$\tilde{r}\approx 0.6$ \citep{zong2023circapower}.


Using a single representative $r$, as in \textsc{CircaPower}, therefore produces
severely over-optimistic power projections for the majority of the transcriptome,
and the per-gene formula \eqref{eq:circapower_formula} does not account for the
multiple-testing correction applied when testing thousands of genes simultaneously.
\textsc{SCP} addresses these limitations through the following four-step
simulation pipeline.



\emph{Step 1: Pilot parameter estimation.}
For each of the $G_0$ pilot genes, fit \eqref{eq:cosinor_model} by OLS to obtain
$(\hat{\mu}_g, \hat{A}_g, \hat{\sigma}_g, \hat{\phi}_g)$ and the corresponding
$p$-value $p_g$ for rhythmicity. To improve stability across datasets with
heterogeneous signal strength, we define a pilot-stage screening rule based on
a fixed marginal threshold $\alpha_{\text{pilot}}$ (default $\alpha_{\text{pilot}}=0.01$),
rather than using multiplicity-adjusted thresholds. Let
$\mathcal{G}_R^{\mathrm{cand}} = \{g : p_g < \alpha_{\text{pilot}}\}$ and
$K_{\mathrm{top}} = \min\bigl(300,\ |\mathcal{G}_R^{\mathrm{cand}}|\bigr)$, and
define the parameter-estimation set as the top-ranked genes
$\mathcal{G}_R = \mathrm{Top}_{K_{\mathrm{top}}}(\mathcal{G}_R^{\mathrm{cand}})$.
The estimated rhythmic proportion is given by
$\hat{\pi}_R = |\mathcal{G}_R^{\mathrm{cand}}| / G_0$.
Empirical distributions are then constructed over the fitted parameters:
\begin{equation}
\hat{F}_\mu
=
\frac{1}{G_0}\sum_{g=1}^{G_0}\delta_{\hat{\mu}_g},
\quad
\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}_R|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_R}\delta_{(\hat{A}_g,\hat{\sigma}_g)},
\quad
\hat{F}_{\phi}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}_R|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_R}\delta_{\hat{\phi}_g}.
\label{eq:empirical_dists}
\end{equation}
The mesor distribution $\hat{F}_\mu$ is estimated from all $G_0$ genes, reflecting a
shared baseline-expression level across the transcriptome regardless of rhythmic status.
We assume a shared empirical mesor distribution across genes, while allowing
gene-specific mesor shifts between groups under the DM state
(Section~\ref{sec:differential}).
Sampling $(A,\sigma)$ jointly from $\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$ preserves the empirical
amplitude-noise dependence, so the induced
$r=A/\sigma$ distribution reflects this relationship without requiring $r$ to be specified separately.
The complete pilot summary $\hat{\Psi} = (\hat{F}_\mu, \hat{F}_{A,\sigma},
\hat{F}_\phi, \hat{F}_{\mathrm{TOD}}, \hat{\pi}_R)$ is passed to the downstream
steps, where $\hat{F}_{\mathrm{TOD}}$ is the empirical collection-time
distribution for passive designs. The choice of $\alpha_{\text{pilot}}$ can be
adjusted by the user. In practice an adequate pilot shares tissue,
condition, and assay platform with the planned study and meets the
identifiability threshold for the intended detector ($B \geq 3$ for
DCP, $B \geq 2K + 1$ for the K-harmonic F-test). The pilot summary
$\hat{\Psi}$ is itself a sample-based estimator: at small
$n_{\mathrm{pilot}}$, residual variability in $\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$ and
$\hat{\pi}_R$ propagates to non-trivial uncertainty in the projected
power curve, which Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap} quantifies through a
nonparametric bootstrap.

\emph{Step 2: Data generation.}
For each simulation replicate $s=1,\ldots,S$ and candidate sample size $N$,
draw rhythmicity indicators $Z_{g,s}^R \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\hat{\pi}_R)$
independently for $g=1,\ldots,G$. Conditional on $Z_{g,s}^R$, observations are
generated from a two-component mixture model
\[
y_{g,i}^{(s)} \mid Z_{g,s}^R \sim
\begin{cases}
M_1\!\left(\mu_g^{(s)}, A_g^{(s)}, \sigma_g^{(s)}, \phi_g^{(s)}\right), & Z_{g,s}^R = 1, \\[6pt]
M_0\!\left(\mu_g^{(s)}, \sigma_g^{(s)}\right), & Z_{g,s}^R = 0,
\end{cases}
\]
where the rhythmic model $M_1$ is the cosinor model
\[
M_1:\quad
y_{g,i}^{(s)} = \mu_g^{(s)} + A_g^{(s)} \cos\!\bigl(\omega_0 t_i + \phi_g^{(s)}\bigr)
+ \varepsilon_{g,i}^{(s)}, \qquad
\varepsilon_{g,i}^{(s)} \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(0, (\sigma_g^{(s)})^2\bigr),
\]
and the arrhythmic model $M_0$ is the reduced intercept-only model
\[
M_0:\quad
y_{g,i}^{(s)} = \mu_g^{(s)} + \varepsilon_{g,i}^{(s)}, \qquad
\varepsilon_{g,i}^{(s)} \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(0, (\sigma_g^{(s)})^2\bigr).
\]

For genes with $Z_{g,s}^R=1$, parameters are sampled as
$\mu_g^{(s)}\sim\hat{F}_\mu$, $(A_g^{(s)},\sigma_g^{(s)})\sim\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$,
and $\phi_g^{(s)}\sim\hat{F}_\phi$. For genes with $Z_{g,s}^R=0$, we set
$A_g^{(s)}=0$, sample $\mu_g^{(s)}\sim\hat{F}_\mu$, and sample $\sigma_g^{(s)}$
from the empirical marginal distribution of $\sigma$ implied by $\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$.

An \emph{active} design specifies $B$ distinct time points with $m_b$ replicates
each and total $n=\sum_b m_b$; a \emph{passive} design draws collection times
from $\hat{F}_{\mathrm{TOD}}$. For balanced equally spaced active designs with
$B\geq 3$, $d(\phi)=1/2$ for all phases, so power depends only on total $N$;
for passive designs the realized $d(\phi_g)$ is computed from the observed
collection times via \eqref{eq:design_factor_circapower}, varies by gene and
replicate, and \textsc{SCP} reports design-factor diagnostics. 




\emph{Step 3: Inference.}
Apply the $F$-test \eqref{eq:rhythm_f_test} to every gene and BH correction at
nominal level $\alpha$ across all $G$ genes. Let $D_{g,s}\in\{0,1\}$ denote
the discovery indicator ($D_{g,s}=1$ if gene $g$ is declared rhythmic in
replicate $s$).

\emph{Step 4: Power aggregation.}
With $Z_{g,s}^R\in\{0,1\}$ the true rhythmicity status assigned in Step~2,
empirical power is
\[
\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}(N)
=
\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^S
\frac{\sum_g D_{g,s}Z_{g,s}^R}{\sum_g Z_{g,s}^R},
\]
and empirical FDR is the per-replicate fraction of false discoveries among all
discoveries, averaged across $S$ replicates. Because parameters are drawn from
the full pilot distributions $\hat{F}_\mu$, $\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$, and $\hat{F}_\phi$
(Step~1), the power estimate integrates over the observed transcriptome-wide
heterogeneity in baseline expression, amplitude, noise, and phase rather than
relying on a single representative effect size. Because BH
correction is applied within each replicate (Step~3), genome-wide multiplicity
control is captured naturally rather than treated as a post-hoc adjustment.
The indicators $D_{g,s}$ and $Z_{g,s}^R$ introduced here carry over directly
to Section~\ref{sec:differential}, where endpoint-specific superscripts
distinguish among differential endpoints.

A computationally efficient alternative to the full simulation pipeline is also available for the single-cohort setting. Equation~\eqref{eq:circapower_formula}
is evaluated gene-by-gene at each pilot gene's estimated
$\hat{r}_g = \hat{A}_g/\hat{\sigma}_g$ and design factor
$\hat{d}(\hat{\phi}_g)$, and the results are averaged to yield
$\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}_{\mathrm{CF}}(N)$.
This approximation is computationally efficient, but it does not propagate
genome-wide FDR or the joint $(A_g,\sigma_g)$ heterogeneity. The full
simulation-based pipeline is therefore recommended when transcriptome-wide
operating characteristics are the primary design target.

\subsection{Power calculation for case-control differential circadian analysis}
\label{sec:differential}

The two-group setting evaluates three biological endpoints: a gene
gains or loses 24-hour rhythmicity between conditions (DR), keeps
rhythmicity but shifts its peak in time (DP), or maintains its
rhythm shape but shifts its mean expression level (DM). Each
endpoint is tested separately within a TOJR-based two-stage
procedure, so the single-group indicators $D_{g,s}$ and $Z_{g,s}^R$
from Section~\ref{sec:single_cohort} generalize to endpoint-indexed
quantities. For each endpoint
$E\in\{\mathrm{DR},\mathrm{DP},\mathrm{DM}\}$,
$D_{g,s}^E\in\{0,1\}$ denotes the endpoint-specific discovery indicator and
$Z_{g,s}^E\in\{0,1\}$ denotes the true differential status. A targeted indicator $Z_{g,s}^{*,E} = Z_{g,s}^E \cdot \mathbf{1}\{\delta_{g,s}^E \geq \Delta_E\}$ restricts attention to genes whose effect size exceeds a user-specified threshold $\Delta_E$, where
$\delta_{g,s}^{\mathrm{DR}} = |R_{g,1,s}^2 - R_{g,2,s}^2|$ is the absolute difference in cosinor goodness-of-fit between groups,
$\delta_{g,s}^{\mathrm{DP}} = \min\!\bigl(|\phi_{g,1,s}-\phi_{g,2,s}|,\,24-|\phi_{g,1,s}-\phi_{g,2,s}|\bigr)$ is the circular phase distance,
and $\delta_{g,s}^{\mathrm{DM}} = |\mu_{g,2,s} - \mu_{g,1,s}|$ is the absolute mesor difference.
Here $R_{g,k,s}^2$ for $k \in \{1, 2\}$ denotes the coefficient of
determination of the cosinor model fit restricted to group $k$, that
is, $R_{g,k,s}^2 = 1 - \mathrm{SSE}_{g,k,s}/\mathrm{SST}_{g,k,s}$,
where $\mathrm{SSE}$ and $\mathrm{SST}$ are the residual and total sums
of squares of the group-$k$ cosinor fit. The group index $k$ used here
is distinct from the harmonic index $k$ in
Section~\ref{sec:cosinor_violation}.

When comparing rhythmic patterns between two groups, inference becomes
multi-stage: genes are first partitioned by per-group rhythmicity, followed by
endpoint-specific tests within data-derived strata. Because the multi-stage inference procedure does not yield tractable analytical
expressions for power and FDR, these quantities are evaluated via simulation
following the full analysis pipeline.

\textsc{SCP} evaluates three endpoints: differential rhythmicity (DR), differential
phase (DP), and differential MESOR (DM). DR captures gain or loss of rhythmicity
(e.g., clock genes that cycle in healthy tissue but lose rhythmicity in disease);
DP captures a shift in peak-expression timing; and DM captures a change in
baseline expression level while rhythmic structure is preserved.
Their simulation proportions are denoted
$(\pi_{\mathrm{DR}}, \pi_R, \pi_{\mathrm{DP}}, \pi_{\mathrm{DM}})$.
When pilot data for both groups are available, $\pi_{\mathrm{DR}}$ and
$\pi_{\mathrm{DP}}$ are estimated empirically from the two-group pilot comparison;
$\pi_{\mathrm{DM}}$ is always user-specified, as mesor differences are not reliably
identifiable from the pilot distributions used to calibrate baseline parameters.
Otherwise all proportions are user-specified, with $\pi_R$ initialized from
$\hat{\pi}_R$ by default. When group-specific pilot data are available, the shared
summary $\hat{\Psi}$ can be replaced by group-specific summaries
$\hat{\Psi}_1$ and $\hat{\Psi}_2$, each estimated from the corresponding pilot,
to capture group-level heterogeneity in baseline parameters.
The simulation follows the same four-step structure as
Section~\ref{sec:single_cohort}, with Step~1 shared.

\emph{Step 2: Two-group data generation.}
For each gene, a baseline parameter tuple
$(\mu_g^{(s)}, A_g^{(s)}, \sigma_g^{(s)}, \phi_g^{(s)})$
is sampled from the pilot summary
$\hat{\Psi}=(\hat{F}_{\mu},\hat{F}_{A,\sigma},\hat{F}_{\phi},\hat{F}_{\mathrm{TOD}})$
constructed in Step~1, with $\hat{F}_\mu$ estimated from all $G_0$ genes and
$\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$, $\hat{F}_\phi$ from the rhythmic set $\mathcal{G}_R$.
A latent type $T_g \in \{0,1,2,3,4,5\}$ is then independently assigned
by sampling from a categorical distribution with type probabilities
\[
P(T_g = 1) = \pi_R, \quad
P(T_g = 2) = P(T_g = 3) = \pi_{\mathrm{DR}} / 2, \quad
P(T_g = 4) = \pi_{\mathrm{DP}}, \quad
P(T_g = 5) = \pi_{\mathrm{DM}},
\]
and
$P(T_g = 0) = 1 - \pi_R - \pi_{\mathrm{DR}} - \pi_{\mathrm{DP}} -
\pi_{\mathrm{DM}}$,
where differential rhythmicity is split equally between loss
($T_g = 2$, rhythmic in group 1 only) and gain ($T_g = 3$, rhythmic in
group 2 only).

Group-specific parameters are initialized by setting
$\mu_{g,1}^{(s)} = \mu_{g,2}^{(s)} = \mu_g^{(s)}$,
$A_{g,1}^{(s)} = A_{g,2}^{(s)} = A_g^{(s)}$, and
$\phi_{g,1}^{(s)} = \phi_{g,2}^{(s)} = \phi_g^{(s)}$, and are then modified
according to $T_g$. Observations are generated as
\[
y_{g,i,k}^{(s)} \sim M_{T_g}(\mu_{g,k}^{(s)}, A_{g,k}^{(s)}, \sigma_g^{(s)}, \phi_{g,k}^{(s)}),
\quad k\in\{1,2\},
\]
where $M_1$ denotes the cosinor model and $M_0$ the intercept-only model,
with Gaussian noise $\varepsilon_{g,i,k}^{(s)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,(\sigma_g^{(s)})^2)$.

Specifically, $T_g=0$ yields arrhythmic genes with $A_{g,1}=A_{g,2}=0$ and
$y_{g,i,k}^{(s)}=\mu_g^{(s)}+\varepsilon_{g,i,k}^{(s)}$ (model $M_0$),
whereas $T_g=1$ yields jointly rhythmic genes with shared parameters across
groups following $M_1$. For differential rhythmicity, $T_g=2$ sets $A_{g,2}=0$
and $T_g=3$ sets $A_{g,1}=0$, so that one group follows $M_1$ and the other
$M_0$. For differential phase ($T_g=4$), both groups follow $M_1$ with
$\phi_{g,2} = (\phi_{g,1} + \Delta \phi_g)\bmod 24$ and
$\Delta \phi_g \sim \mathrm{Unif}(\Delta \phi_{\min}, \Delta \phi_{\max})$;
for differential MESOR ($T_g=5$), both groups follow $M_1$ with
$\mu_{g,2} = \mu_{g,1} + \Delta \mu_g$ and
$\Delta \mu_g \sim \mathrm{Unif}(\Delta \mu_{\min}, \Delta \mu_{\max})$.

\emph{Step 3: DCP inference.}
Inference proceeds via \textsc{DiffCircaPipeline}. For each group $k$,
cosinor models are fit and $p$-values $p_{g,k}$ are obtained. Genes are
assigned Test-of-Joint-Rhythmicity (TOJR) labels
\citep{xue2023diffcircapipeline}:
\[
\hat{Z}_g =
\begin{cases}
\texttt{arrhy}   & p_{g,1}>\alpha \text{ and } p_{g,2}>\alpha,\\
\texttt{g1-only} & p_{g,1}\leq\alpha \text{ and } p_{g,2}>\alpha,\\
\texttt{g2-only} & p_{g,1}>\alpha \text{ and } p_{g,2}\leq\alpha,\\
\texttt{both}    & p_{g,1}\leq\alpha \text{ and } p_{g,2}\leq\alpha.
\end{cases}
\]
where $\alpha$ denotes the inference-stage threshold, distinct from the
pilot-stage threshold $\alpha_{\text{pilot}}$ used in Step~1. Endpoint-specific
tests are then applied within strata, followed by BH correction at level $\alpha$.

\emph{Step 4: Power and FDR summarization.}
For each endpoint $E$, let $R_s^E=\sum_g D_{g,s}^E$ and
$V_s^E=\sum_g D_{g,s}^E(1-Z_{g,s}^E)$. The per-replicate FDR is
$\widehat{\mathrm{FDR}}_s^E = V_s^E/R_s^E$ when $R_s^E>0$, and 0 otherwise.
Targeted power is
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{TPower}}_s^E(\Delta_E)
=
\frac{\sum_g D_{g,s}^E Z_{g,s}^{*,E}}{\sum_g Z_{g,s}^{*,E}},
\label{eq:tpower_def}
\end{equation}
where the dependence on $\Delta_E$ enters through the targeted
indicator $Z_{g,s}^{*,E} = Z_{g,s}^E \cdot \mathbf{1}\{\delta_{g,s}^E
\geq \Delta_E\}$ defined above. Final summaries are obtained by
averaging across simulation replicates.

\subsection{Bootstrap uncertainty in projected power}
\label{sec:bootstrap}

The procedure in Section~\ref{sec:single_cohort} is plug-in: the pilot summary
$\hat{\Psi}$ is estimated once and treated as fixed when computing the projected
power curve $\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}(N)$. When the pilot cohort is small,
however, the estimated amplitude--noise distribution
$\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$ and rhythmic proportion $\hat{\pi}_R$ may vary
substantially across pilot samples, leading to uncertainty in the resulting
design recommendation.

To quantify this uncertainty, \textsc{SCP} uses an outer nonparametric
bootstrap over pilot subjects \citep{efron1979bootstrap}. We describe the
procedure in the single-cohort setting for concreteness; the differential
setting in Section~\ref{sec:differential} uses the same outer resampling and
inner simulation structure, applied to endpoint-specific targeted power curves
rather than $\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}(N)$. For each bootstrap replicate
$b = 1, \ldots, B_{\mathrm{out}}$: \emph{(i)} resample subjects with replacement
from the pilot cohort; \emph{(ii)} recompute the pilot summary
$\hat{\Psi}^{(b)}$; and \emph{(iii)} run the full inner simulation pipeline to
obtain $\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}^{(b)}(N)$.

The bootstrap mean
$\bar{\mathrm{Power}}(N)
=
B_{\mathrm{out}}^{-1}
\sum_{b=1}^{B_{\mathrm{out}}}
\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}^{(b)}(N)$,
together with the empirical 2.5\% and 97.5\% quantiles of
$\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}^{(b)}(N)$, provides a bootstrap confidence band
reflecting uncertainty in the pilot-derived empirical distributions and
rhythmic proportion contained in $\hat{\Psi}$. The procedure is nested:
outer resampling captures pilot-to-pilot variability, whereas inner simulations
capture gene-level Monte Carlo variability conditional on the resampled pilot.

The bootstrap should therefore be interpreted as quantifying uncertainty in the
design recommendation rather than correcting finite-sample bias. In very small
or weakly rhythmic pilots, both the plug-in estimate and the bootstrap mean may
remain unstable because the pilot-derived empirical distributions are themselves
estimated with limited information. In larger, well-powered pilots
($n_{\mathrm{pilot}} \gtrsim 250$), the plug-in curve is typically stable and
the bootstrap band mainly serves as a sensitivity diagnostic.

\subsection{A two-harmonic cosinor extension}
\label{sec:cosinor_violation}

The single-harmonic model in \eqref{eq:cosinor_model} captures only the fundamental 24-hour component. A second 12-hour harmonic captures a substantial fraction of the remaining within-day variation in circadian transcriptomic profiles \citep{hughes2009harmonics,zhu201712hour}.

A two-harmonic cosinor model is
\begin{equation}
y_{gi}
=
\mu_g
+
\sum_{k=1}^{2}
A_{g,k}\cos \bigl(k\omega_0 (t_i - \phi_{g,k})\bigr)
+
\varepsilon_{gi},
\qquad
\varepsilon_{gi}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}N(0,\sigma_g^2),
\label{eq:twoharm_model}
\end{equation}
with fundamental component $(A_{g,1},\phi_{g,1})$ and second harmonic $(A_{g,2},\phi_{g,2})$. The restriction $A_{g,2}=0$ yields \eqref{eq:cosinor_model}. Estimation proceeds through the equivalent linear form
\[
y_{gi}
=
\mu_g
+
\sum_{k=1}^{2}
\bigl[
a_{g,k}\cos(k\omega_0 t_i)
+
b_{g,k}\sin(k\omega_0 t_i)
\bigr]
+
\varepsilon_{gi},
\]
where $A_{g,k} = \sqrt{a_{g,k}^2 + b_{g,k}^2}$.

Rhythmicity is tested by $H_0:A_{g,1}=A_{g,2}=0$, equivalently $a_{g,1}=b_{g,1}=a_{g,2}=b_{g,2}=0$. Relative to \eqref{eq:rhythm_f_test}, this increases the numerator degrees of freedom from $2$ to $4$:
\begin{equation}
F_g
=
\frac{(\mathrm{TSS}_g - \mathrm{RSS}_g)/4}{\mathrm{RSS}_g/(N-5)}
\sim F(4,\, N-5).
\label{eq:twoharm_f_test}
\end{equation}
BH correction is applied across genes as in Section~\ref{sec:single_cohort}; at $K=1$, \eqref{eq:twoharm_f_test} reduces to \eqref{eq:rhythm_f_test}.

Under $H_A$, \eqref{eq:twoharm_f_test} is noncentral $F(4,\,N-5,\,\lambda_g)$, so power is given by \eqref{eq:circapower_formula} with $(2,N-3)$ replaced by $(4,N-5)$. For balanced designs, orthogonality of the harmonic basis yields additive signal contributions:
\begin{equation}
\lambda_g
=
N d \bigl(r_{g,1}^2 + r_{g,2}^2\bigr)
=
\frac{N}{2} r_{g,\mathrm{eff}}^2,
\qquad
r_{g,k} = \frac{A_{g,k}}{\sigma_g},
\qquad
r_{g,\mathrm{eff}}^2 = r_{g,1}^2 + r_{g,2}^2,
\label{eq:twoharm_ncp}
\end{equation}
with $d=1/2$ for balanced equally spaced active designs. Thus $\lambda_g$ depends on the standardized harmonic amplitudes through $r_{g,\mathrm{eff}}$ and, under the active balanced design, not on $(\phi_{g,1},\phi_{g,2})$, paralleling \eqref{eq:ncp}.

For $K=2$, the model has $2K+1=5$ free parameters. Full column rank therefore requires at least five distinct sampling phases per period; otherwise the regression is rank-deficient and the $F$-test is conservative or undefined. Hence an active design must satisfy $B\geq 5$. This corresponds to the Nyquist--Shannon identifiability condition for trigonometric regression \citep{Nyquist1928,Shannon1949}, consistent with recommendations for circadian transcriptomic designs \citep{Hughes2017guidelines}. In passive designs the condition is typically met because collection times are distinct; in active designs it constrains the sampling grid.

We use $K=2$ by default. This includes the dominant ultradian second harmonic while preserving identifiability whenever $B\geq 5$; larger $K$ increases the identifiability bound to $B\geq 2K+1$ and typically yields diminishing returns beyond the second harmonic.

On the \textsc{GTEx} Liver pilot, allowing a second harmonic substantially broadened rhythmic gene recovery at BH-FDR $0.05$ (Figure~\ref{fig:twoharm_demo}). The two-harmonic-only set was led by KEGG enrichment for lysosome biogenesis, with $35$ genes and adjusted $p \approx 3.9\times 10^{-6}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:twoharm_demo}C). That coherence argues that the extra detections reflect a real rhythmic subspace with non-sinusoidal shape, not random expansion of the called set across the transcriptome.

Under balanced active sampling on the Ketchesin Putamen control pilot, power was effectively determined by total sample size once the design cleared the identifiability bound, not by how samples were partitioned across time points (Figure~\ref{fig:active_vs_passive}). For the single-harmonic test, where $B \geq 3$, the six grids $B \in \{3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24\}$ overlapped within about $1\%$ at every $N$, and each reached $80\%$ power near $N = 50$. The same pattern held for the two-harmonic test after identifiability, with $B \geq 5$: the admissible grids $B \in \{6, 8, 12, 24\}$ overlapped within about $1.5\%$ and also reached $80\%$ power near $N = 50$, while $B \in \{3, 4\}$ were rank-deficient and undefined. This is the practical manifestation of the phase-independent noncentrality in \eqref{eq:twoharm_ncp} and \eqref{eq:ncp}: under a balanced equally spaced design with $B$ above $2K + 1$, $\lambda_g$ depends on $N$ and $r_{g,\mathrm{eff}}$ rather than on the allocation $(B, m)$.

What did differ was the cost of fitting unnecessary harmonic degrees of freedom on a near-cosinor pilot. At $N = 16$, the achieved power was approximately $35\%$ for $K = 1$ and $27\%$ for $K = 2$. At $N = 20$, it was approximately $46\%$ for $K = 1$ and $40\%$ for $K = 2$. That gap closed by $N \approx 30$, after which the two curves coincided within sampling error. The reason is straightforward: in \eqref{eq:twoharm_f_test}, the second-harmonic test uses two additional numerator degrees of freedom, and on this pilot those extra parameters return little noncentrality because 12-hour structure is weak. The design implication is therefore simple. Once the identifiability bound is met, $B \geq 3$ for $K = 1$ and $B \geq 5$ for $K = 2$, the choice between $B$ and $m$ reduces to the choice of total $N$; detector performance is then driven by waveform shape, not by the sampling grid or by the active-versus-passive distinction itself.

\section{Discussion}
%% ====================================================================

Across the pilot datasets in our test set, passive-design $\tilde{r}$ spans roughly a two-fold range, from approximately $0.48$ for \textsc{GTEx} Liver to approximately $1.06$ for the Ketchesin Putamen control cohort, and that shift alone is enough to move the required sample size by more than a factor of four. A concrete contrast appears between \textsc{GTEx} Adrenal Gland and \textsc{GTEx} Liver: at \textsc{GTEx} Adrenal Gland ($\tilde{r}\approx 0.97$, passive), the interpolated $N_{80}$ at FDR $0.05$ is approximately $49$; at \textsc{GTEx} Liver ($\tilde{r}\approx 0.48$, passive), $N_{80}$ exceeds the $N \leq 200$ simulation grid (Figure~\ref{fig:sc_adrliv}). The implication is straightforward: even with the same pipeline, detector, and FDR threshold, planning targets must be calibrated to the underlying effect-size distribution. This reproduces the core message of \textsc{PROPER} for RNA-seq \citep{wu2015proper} in the circadian setting and argues against generic sample-size recommendations.

Differential analysis carries a clear sample-size penalty relative to single-cohort detection. The main reason is that differential rhythmicity, phase, and MESOR testing is conditional on a data-derived joint rhythmicity classification, which both breaks analytic tractability and adds noise before the endpoint-specific test is even evaluated. In the passive \textsc{GTEx} Adrenal Gland versus Liver comparison (Figure~\ref{fig:diff_adrliv}), the per-group $N_{80}$ at FDR $0.20$ is approximately $120$ for differential phase, rising to roughly $190$ for both differential rhythmicity and differential MESOR. That ordering is informative rather than incidental: the phase-shift effect-size distribution is the most concentrated in this comparison, whereas the rhythmicity and MESOR endpoints remain comparably difficult. Relative to the matched single-cohort $N_{80}$, the two-stage cost is roughly two- to fivefold, with the widest gap at low $\tilde{r}$, where the classification step is noisiest.

The bootstrap of Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap} is most useful as a way to expose how much the pilot itself limits confidence in projected power, not as a correction for plug-in bias. Figure~\ref{fig:bootstrap_sc} makes that contrast explicit across $n_{\mathrm{pilot}} \in \{28, 416\}$. At the small-pilot end, the widest $95\%$ confidence band occurs for Putamen schizophrenia controls, where $n_{\mathrm{pilot}} = 28$. At the large-pilot end, the band narrows to the inner-Monte-Carlo noise floor for \textsc{GTEx} Thyroid, where $n_{\mathrm{pilot}} = 416$. In practice, that distinction matters because, at the small pilot, the band can span the difference between an underpowered and an adequately powered design at a fixed $N$, a distinction the plug-in point estimate cannot make. For intermediate pilots ($40 < n_{\mathrm{pilot}} < 250$), the bootstrap band is best viewed as a sanity check on the plug-in projection. For large pilots ($n_{\mathrm{pilot}} \gtrsim 250$), the plug-in recommendation is faithful and the bootstrap is optional.

When the pilot waveform departs from a single sinusoid, the two-harmonic test recovers signal that the single-harmonic analysis leaves behind. On \textsc{GTEx} Liver, the second-harmonic test detected $518$ genes not declared by the fundamental-only test. The reverse direction contributed only $3$ genes, and the added discoveries formed a functionally coherent set (Figure~\ref{fig:twoharm_demo}). The associated power behavior follows directly from the noncentrality of \eqref{eq:twoharm_ncp}: marginal power rises monotonically in both $N$ and the FDR target. When the simulations are stratified by $r_{\mathrm{eff}} = \sqrt{r_1^2 + r_2^2}$, the power curves order exactly as the noncentrality predicts, with saturation above $r_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 0.75$ and negligible power in the lowest stratum (Figure~\ref{fig:twoharm_power}). These are empirical simulation results that instantiate the closed-form scaling derived in Section~\ref{sec:cosinor_violation}, rather than independent theoretical claims.

Harmonic order primarily determines model complexity, whereas
time-sampling allocation governs temporal coverage of the design. Under
a balanced active design, power is approximately invariant to the number
of sampling times $B$ at fixed total sample size $N$, provided that $B$
satisfies the harmonic-regression identifiability condition
$B \geq 2K + 1$ ($B \geq 3$ for $K = 1$ and $B \geq 5$ for $K = 2$).
This behavior is consistent with the phase-independent noncentrality in
\eqref{eq:ncp} and \eqref{eq:twoharm_ncp}. In the near-cosinor Putamen
control pilot, the power curves for different sampling grids overlapped
within about $1.5\%$ for both harmonic orders, and both reached $80\%$
power near $N = 50$ (Figure~\ref{fig:active_vs_passive}).

The main cost of the richer model was visible only at small $N$. In the
same pilot, the two-harmonic test was modestly less powerful than the
single-harmonic test, for example approximately $27\%$ versus $35\%$ at
$N = 16$. This loss reflects the expected degrees-of-freedom penalty
when the data contain little additional second-harmonic signal. Thus,
the two-harmonic model provides a useful extension: it can capture
ultradian structure when present, remains identifiable for balanced
designs with $B \geq 5$, and has limited cost once the design is
moderately sized. Above this identifiability threshold, the classical
$B$-versus-$m$ trade-off emphasized in design guidance
\citep{Hughes2017guidelines} is largely reduced to a question of total
sample size $N$.

Active and passive designs, despite their different efficiencies, are handled within the same estimation and inference framework. The balanced active design achieves a noncentrality $\lambda_g = N r_g^2 / 2$, which is twice the value of an unstructured passive design with the same $N$ (design factor $d = 1/2$ versus $d \approx 0.24$ for typical postmortem collection-time distributions). Passive designs pay that efficiency cost, but they remain unavoidable for human postmortem brain, clinical-cohort chronobiology, and any setting in which collection time is set by external scheduling rather than experimental protocol. The practical point is that neither the detector nor the FDR control needs to change across those settings. Across all of these analyses, the key input is the pilot-derived effect-size distribution, which is why \textsc{SCP} calibrates power to a pilot rather than to a nominal effect size.

\subsection{A decision tree for circadian study planning}
\label{sec:decision_tree}

We close with a four-step rule for using \textsc{SCP} prospectively.

\textbf{Step 1, detector.} Match the detector to waveform shape first. If the pilot waveform departs appreciably from a single sinusoid and the planned design will satisfy $B \geq 5$, use the two-harmonic $F$-test; otherwise use the single-harmonic cosinor $F$-test. The active-versus-passive distinction does not govern this choice. What matters is whether the waveform requires the additional harmonic, and the two-harmonic test reduces to the single harmonic at $K = 1$.

\textbf{Step 2, sampling grid.} Once the detector is chosen, the sampling grid only needs to clear the identifiability threshold. Choose any $B$ at or above the harmonic-regression identifiability threshold $B \geq 2K + 1$, that is, $B \geq 3$ for the single-harmonic test and $B \geq 5$ for the two-harmonic test. Above that threshold, the $(B, m)$ allocation does not affect power at fixed $N$, so the design problem reduces to selecting total $N$. Both detectors are also available as stand-alone tests subject to the same identifiability requirement on the supplied design.

\textbf{Step 3, sample size.} Sample-size planning should be read directly from the pilot-calibrated power curve. Use $N_{80}$ at the pilot $\tilde{r}$, with a closed-form calculation for the single-cohort setting in Section~\ref{sec:single_cohort} and a simulation-based calculation for the differential setting in Section~\ref{sec:differential}. In our pilots, a passive moderately rhythmic tissue with $\tilde{r}\approx 1.0$ typically yields $N_{80} \approx 50$. A passive low-$\tilde{r}$ tissue with $\tilde{r}\lesssim 0.5$ instead gives $N_{80} > 200$. Differential analyses should be planned per group: on the passive \textsc{GTEx} Adrenal Gland versus Liver comparison, the FDR-$0.20$ per-group $N_{80}$ is approximately $120$ for differential phase, $190$ for differential rhythmicity, and $190$ for differential MESOR.

\textbf{Step 4, bootstrap.} Use the bootstrap when pilot uncertainty is large enough to change the design decision. If $n_{\mathrm{pilot}} \leq 40$, run the outer subject-bootstrap of Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap} and report the $95\%$ confidence band alongside the plug-in curve; at this pilot size the bootstrap is essential because it surfaces uncertainty that the plug-in curve alone hides. For intermediate pilots ($40 < n_{\mathrm{pilot}} < 250$), the band is a useful sanity check. For $n_{\mathrm{pilot}} \gtrsim 250$, the plug-in curve is a faithful summary and the bootstrap is optional.

Several limitations apply. The single- and two-harmonic $F$-tests assume Gaussian residuals and degrade on heavily sharp-peaked, near-arrhythmic, or heteroskedastic genes. The differential simulator treats gene types as mutually exclusive, so compound rhythmicity-plus-phase changes are not directly evaluated. Passive-design power depends on the pilot's empirical collection-time distribution, which may not generalize to a planned cohort with different recruitment logistics. The bootstrap is computationally intensive and is recommended only when the analysis above flags pilot uncertainty as material.

\textsc{SCP} is available as an open-source R framework.
