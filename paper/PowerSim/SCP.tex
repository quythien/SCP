\begin{abstract}
Circadian disruption is a hallmark of numerous human diseases, yet detecting condition-specific changes in gene expression rhythms remains a significant challenge. Circadian transcriptomic studies aim to identify rhythmic biomarkers within a given condition or to characterize differential circadian biomarkers across conditions. Given the constraints of limited budgets and sample availability, rigorous power calculation and thoughtful study design are critical for planning such studies. Existing approaches, such as CircaPower, offer closed-form analytical solutions for circadian biomarker detection but do not account for gene-specific signal-to-noise ratios or genome-wide false discovery. To address these limitations, we present SCP (Simulation-based Circadian Power), a simulation-based framework for the study design of circadian transcriptomic analyses. Using gene-specific information from pilot or publicly available data, SCP enables pilot-calibrated, FDR-controlled power calculations across the transcriptome. The framework supports sample size determination for circadian biomarker detection and accommodates multi-faceted differential rhythm analyses, including differential rhythmicity, differential phase, and differential MESOR. For active experimental designs such as in animal studies, we further investigate the trade-off between sampling frequency and biological replicates and resolve it analytically: above the harmonic-regression identifiability threshold, power depends on total sample size alone. For pilots whose waveforms deviate from a pure cosinor, SCP also provides a K-harmonic F-test that generalises the cosinor test to user-tunable harmonic order, stacking $K$ periodic bases and testing the joint harmonic null with a nested F-statistic. Additionally, a bootstrap procedure is introduced to quantify uncertainty in power curves and sample size recommendations. SCP is evaluated on simulated and real circadian datasets across active and passive design regimes.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Circadian rhythms, endogenous $\sim$24-hour oscillations, play a fundamental role in physiology and disease \citep{takahashi2017transcriptional}. Disruption of circadian timing has been implicated in aging and neuropsychiatric disorders \citep{chen2016aging}, and rhythmic transcription regulates metabolism, synaptic function, and immune signaling \citep{takahashi2017transcriptional,mure2018diurnal,chen2016aging}. To unveil circadian regulation in transcriptomic data, studies generally pursue one of two goals: detecting potential circadian biomarkers within a single condition or characterizing differences in rhythmic patterns across conditions, including differential rhythmicity (DR), differential phase (DP), and differential MESOR (i.e., rhythm-adjusted mean expression) (DM). A growing number of methods have been developed for both settings, including rhythmicity detection methods such as \textsc{JTK\_CYCLE}, \textsc{RAIN}, and \textsc{BIO\_CYCLE} for single-condition studies \citep{Hughes2010jtkcycle,Thaben2014rain,Agostinelli2016biocycle}, and differential frameworks such as \textsc{CircaCompare}, \textsc{LimoRhyde}, and \textsc{DiffCircaPipeline} for cross-condition analysis \citep{parsons2020circacompare,Singer2019LimoRhyde,xue2023diffcircapipeline}. While these methods enable statistical inference on circadian signals, they do not address how studies should be designed to achieve reliable detection.

Prospective study design therefore remains a critical bottleneck. Investigators must determine the sample size required to achieve adequate statistical power, assess how sensitive those recommendations are to the limitations of pilot data, and, in active experimental designs, strike the right balance between sampling frequency and biological replicates. In passive observational settings, such as human post-mortem studies, sampling time cannot be controlled and sample acquisition is costly, making principled sample size calculation especially important. Without rigorous design guidance, circadian transcriptomic studies risk being substantially underpowered despite considerable experimental investment.

To our knowledge, \textsc{CircaPower} \citep{zong2023circapower} is currently the only dedicated framework for power and sample size determination in circadian transcriptomic studies. However, it is restricted to single-condition rhythmicity detection and does not extend to differential rhythmicity analysis. CircaPower derives closed-form power calculations under a cosinor model using a fixed intrinsic effect size \( r = A/\sigma \), quantifying per-gene statistical power for detecting rhythmicity. While this provides an important statistical foundation for circadian biomarker discovery, the formulation assumes a uniform effect size across genes and does not account for genome-wide false discovery. In practice, however, the intrinsic effect size \( r \) varies substantially across tissues, species, and genes, with marked differences between core clock genes and the broader transcriptome \citep{zong2023circapower}. Consequently, the fixed-effect-size assumption fails to capture the heterogeneity in signal strength across the genome. Furthermore, because circadian studies operate at the transcriptome-wide scale, false discovery rate (FDR) control is essential. The fixed-\( r \) formulation does not naturally accommodate this requirement, as it lacks a mechanism to model cross-gene variability, a necessary ingredient for characterizing genome-wide error rates.

For power calculation of differential circadian biomarker detection, closed-form analytical solutions analogous to those in CircaPower are generally infeasible. As established in DiffCircaPipeline \citep{xue2023diffcircapipeline}, circadian rhythm disruptions are complex and multi-faceted, encompassing differential rhythmicity (DR), differential phase (DP), and differential MESOR (DM). Detection of these features requires a two-stage workflow contingent on the rhythmicity status inferred for each condition. From a statistical standpoint, this biological complexity involves multiple endpoints coupled with genome-wide multiple testing, and therefore cannot be reduced to a single test statistic or a tractable closed-form solution.

These gaps motivate the development of \textbf{SCP} (Simulation-based Circadian Power), a simulation-based framework for power calculation and study design that supports practical decision-making in planning circadian transcriptomic studies. The framework is organised in five methodological pieces. Section~\ref{sec:single_cohort} develops per-gene power for circadian biomarker detection in a single cohort, with parameter distributions calibrated to pilot data and BH control of the genome-wide false discovery rate. Section~\ref{sec:differential} extends the construction to multi-faceted differential rhythm analysis in a two-group setting, covering differential rhythmicity (DR), differential phase (DP), and differential MESOR (DM). Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap} introduces an outer bootstrap that propagates pilot-estimation uncertainty into the projected power curves, a source of variability that plug-in power calculations omit. Section~\ref{sec:cosinor_violation} addresses cosinor violation: we derive the K-harmonic linearisation of the frequency-modulated M\"{o}bius (FMM) model \citep{rueda2019fmm} and implement the resulting test as a K-harmonic F-test with empirical-Bayes variance shrinkage at moderate gene counts. Section~\ref{sec:active_passive} contrasts active and passive designs under the two detectors and identifies when each is preferable. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports simulations and real-data illustrations that benchmark the framework end-to-end.

\subsection{Power calculation for circadian biomarker detection in single cohort}
\label{sec:single_cohort}

For a single cohort of $n$ subjects with sample collection time-of-day (TOD)
$t_1,\ldots,t_n\in[0,24)$, the expression of gene $g$ follows the cosinor
model \citep{cornelissen2014cosinor}
\begin{equation}
y_{gi}
=
\mu_g
+
A_g\cos \bigl(\omega_0 (t_i - \phi_g)\bigr)
+
\varepsilon_{gi},
\qquad
\varepsilon_{gi}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}N(0,\sigma_g^2),
\label{eq:cosinor_model}
\end{equation}
where $\omega_0 = 2\pi/24$ is the angular frequency of the $24$-hour cycle (the cosinor subscript distinguishes it from the FMM shape parameter $\omega \in (0, 1]$ introduced in Section~\ref{sec:cosinor_violation}), $\mu_g$ is the MESOR (Midline Estimating Statistic Of Rhythm; i.e., rhythm-adjusted mean expression), $A_g\geq 0$ is the amplitude,
$\phi_g\in[0,24)$ is the acrophase, and $\sigma_g$ is the noise level. The CircaPower paper by Zong et al. \cite{zong2023circapower} established the theoretical foundations of power calculation of circadian biomarker detection below under the cosinor model with Gaussian errors. To test for rhythmicity of gene $g$, $F$-statistic for $H_0: A_g = 0$ versus $H_A: A_g > 0$ was considered: 
\begin{equation}
F_g
=
\frac{(\mathrm{TSS}_g - \mathrm{RSS}_g)/2}{\mathrm{RSS}_g/(n-3)}
\sim F(2,\, n-3),
\label{eq:rhythm_f_test}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{RSS}_g$ and $\mathrm{TSS}_g$ are the residual and total sums of
squares from the cosinor fit and $F(a,\, b)$ is F-distribution with degrees of freedom $a$ and $b$. Across $G$ genes, Benjamini--Hochberg (BH)
correction \citep{benjamini1995controlling} at nominal FDR level $\alpha$
controls FDR for rhythmicity detection.

For power calculation, under the alternative hypothesis $H_A$, the $F$-statistic follows a noncentral $F$-distribution:
$F_g\sim  F\bigl( 2,\, n-3, \, \lambda_g\bigr)$ with degrees of freedom $2$ and $n-3$ and noncentrality parameter $\lambda_g$. The per-gene statistical power at sample size $n$ is then
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Power}_g(n)
=
1 - F\bigl(q_{\alpha}; 2,\, n-3, \, \lambda_g\bigr),
\label{eq:circapower_formula}
\end{equation}

\noindent where $q_{\alpha}=F(1-\alpha;\,2,\,n-3)$ is the rejection threshold under $H_0$ at level $\alpha$ and $F(x; a,b)$ is the CDF of F-distribution $F(2, n-3)$; $F(x; a,b,\lambda)$ is the CDF of noncentral F-distribution $F(a,b,\lambda)$. 

Zong et al.(2024) showed that 
\begin{equation}
\lambda_g = r_g^2 \cdot n \cdot d(\phi_g),
\label{eq:ncp}
\end{equation}
where $r_g = A_g/\sigma_g$ is the intrinsic effect size of gene $g$, and the design factor $d(\phi_g)$ captures how well the sample collection TOD $(t_1,\cdots, t_n)$ spread on time domain:
\begin{equation}
d(\phi_g)
=
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \cos^2\bigl(\omega_0 (t_i - \phi_g)\bigr)
-
\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \cos\bigl(\omega_0 (t_i - \phi_g)\bigr)\right)^{\!2}.
\label{eq:design_factor_circapower}
\end{equation}

% Brief B-invariance statement with a forward pointer; full derivation
% is reserved for Section~\ref{sec:active_passive} and Supplementary~S6.
For balanced equally spaced active designs with $B\geq 3$ time points,
$d(\phi_g)=1/2$ for all phases, so \eqref{eq:ncp} reduces to
$\lambda_g = r_g^2 n / 2$ and power depends only on $r_g$ and $n$; see
Section~\ref{sec:active_passive} and Supplementary~S6.

In transcriptome-wide studies, however, $r_g$ varies substantially across
genes: core clock genes in mouse liver typically have $r \approx 3$--$6$,
while the median $r$ across all expressed genes in human brain tissue is
$\tilde{r}\approx 0.6$ \citep{zong2023circapower}.


Using a single representative $r$, as in \textsc{CircaPower}, therefore produces
severely over-optimistic power projections for the majority of the transcriptome,
and the per-gene formula \eqref{eq:circapower_formula} does not account for the
multiple-testing correction applied when testing thousands of genes simultaneously.
\textsc{SCP} addresses these limitations through the following four-step
simulation pipeline.



\emph{Step 1: Pilot parameter estimation.}
For each of the $G_0$ pilot genes, fit \eqref{eq:cosinor_model} by OLS to obtain
$(\hat{\mu}_g, \hat{A}_g, \hat{\sigma}_g, \hat{\phi}_g)$ and the corresponding
$p$-value $p_g$ for rhythmicity. To improve stability across datasets with
heterogeneous signal strength, we define a pilot-stage screening rule based on
a fixed marginal threshold $\alpha_{\text{pilot}}$ (default $\alpha_{\text{pilot}}=0.01$),
rather than using multiplicity-adjusted thresholds. Let
$\mathcal{G}_R^{\mathrm{cand}} = \{g : p_g < \alpha_{\text{pilot}}\}$ and
$K_{\mathrm{top}} = \min\bigl(300,\ |\mathcal{G}_R^{\mathrm{cand}}|\bigr)$, and
define the parameter-estimation set as the top-ranked genes
$\mathcal{G}_R = \mathrm{Top}_{K_{\mathrm{top}}}(\mathcal{G}_R^{\mathrm{cand}})$.
The estimated rhythmic proportion is given by
$\hat{\pi}_R = |\mathcal{G}_R^{\mathrm{cand}}| / G_0$.
Empirical distributions are then constructed over the fitted parameters:
\begin{equation}
\hat{F}_\mu
=
\frac{1}{G_0}\sum_{g=1}^{G_0}\delta_{\hat{\mu}_g},
\quad
\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}_R|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_R}\delta_{(\hat{A}_g,\hat{\sigma}_g)},
\quad
\hat{F}_{\phi}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}_R|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_R}\delta_{\hat{\phi}_g}.
\label{eq:empirical_dists}
\end{equation}
The mesor distribution $\hat{F}_\mu$ is estimated from all $G_0$ genes, reflecting a
shared baseline-expression level across the transcriptome regardless of rhythmic status.
We assume a shared empirical mesor distribution across genes, while allowing
gene-specific mesor shifts between groups under the DM state
(Section~\ref{sec:differential}).
Sampling $(A,\sigma)$ jointly from $\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$ preserves the empirical
amplitude-noise dependence, so the induced
$r=A/\sigma$ distribution reflects this relationship without requiring $r$ to be specified separately.
The complete pilot summary $\hat{\Psi} = (\hat{F}_\mu, \hat{F}_{A,\sigma},
\hat{F}_\phi, \hat{F}_{\mathrm{TOD}}, \hat{\pi}_R)$ is passed to the downstream
steps, where $\hat{F}_{\mathrm{TOD}}$ is the empirical collection-time
distribution for passive designs. The choice of $\alpha_{\text{pilot}}$ can be
adjusted by the user. In practice an adequate pilot shares tissue,
condition, and assay platform with the planned study and meets the
identifiability threshold for the intended detector ($B \geq 3$ for
DCP, $B \geq 2K + 1$ for the K-harmonic F-test). The pilot summary
$\hat{\Psi}$ is itself a sample-based estimator: at small
$n_{\mathrm{pilot}}$, residual variability in $\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$ and
$\hat{\pi}_R$ propagates to non-trivial uncertainty in the projected
power curve, which Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap} quantifies through a
nonparametric bootstrap.

\emph{Step 2: Data generation.}
For each simulation replicate $s=1,\ldots,S$ and candidate sample size $n$,
draw rhythmicity indicators $Z_{g,s}^R \sim \mathrm{Bernoulli}(\hat{\pi}_R)$
independently for $g=1,\ldots,G$. Conditional on $Z_{g,s}^R$, observations are
generated from a two-component mixture model
\[
y_{g,i}^{(s)} \mid Z_{g,s}^R \sim
\begin{cases}
M_1\!\left(\mu_g^{(s)}, A_g^{(s)}, \sigma_g^{(s)}, \phi_g^{(s)}\right), & Z_{g,s}^R = 1, \\[6pt]
M_0\!\left(\mu_g^{(s)}, \sigma_g^{(s)}\right), & Z_{g,s}^R = 0,
\end{cases}
\]
where the rhythmic model $M_1$ is the cosinor model
\[
M_1:\quad
y_{g,i}^{(s)} = \mu_g^{(s)} + A_g^{(s)} \cos\!\bigl(\omega_0 t_i + \phi_g^{(s)}\bigr)
+ \varepsilon_{g,i}^{(s)}, \qquad
\varepsilon_{g,i}^{(s)} \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(0, (\sigma_g^{(s)})^2\bigr),
\]
and the arrhythmic model $M_0$ is the reduced intercept-only model
\[
M_0:\quad
y_{g,i}^{(s)} = \mu_g^{(s)} + \varepsilon_{g,i}^{(s)}, \qquad
\varepsilon_{g,i}^{(s)} \sim \mathcal{N}\bigl(0, (\sigma_g^{(s)})^2\bigr).
\]

For genes with $Z_{g,s}^R=1$, parameters are sampled as
$\mu_g^{(s)}\sim\hat{F}_\mu$, $(A_g^{(s)},\sigma_g^{(s)})\sim\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$,
and $\phi_g^{(s)}\sim\hat{F}_\phi$. For genes with $Z_{g,s}^R=0$, we set
$A_g^{(s)}=0$, sample $\mu_g^{(s)}\sim\hat{F}_\mu$, and sample $\sigma_g^{(s)}$
from the empirical marginal distribution of $\sigma$ implied by $\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$.

An \emph{active} design specifies $B$ distinct time points with $m_b$ replicates
each and total $n=\sum_b m_b$; a \emph{passive} design draws collection times
from $\hat{F}_{\mathrm{TOD}}$. For balanced equally spaced active designs with
$B\geq 3$, $d(\phi)=1/2$ for all phases, so power depends only on total $n$;
for passive designs the realized $d(\phi_g)$ is computed from the observed
collection times via \eqref{eq:design_factor_circapower}, varies by gene and
replicate, and \textsc{SCP} reports design-factor diagnostics. 




\emph{Step 3: Inference.}
Apply the $F$-test \eqref{eq:rhythm_f_test} to every gene and BH correction at
nominal level $\alpha$ across all $G$ genes. Let $D_{g,s}\in\{0,1\}$ denote
the discovery indicator ($D_{g,s}=1$ if gene $g$ is declared rhythmic in
replicate $s$).

\emph{Step 4: Power aggregation.}
With $Z_{g,s}^R\in\{0,1\}$ the true rhythmicity status assigned in Step~2,
empirical power is
\[
\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}(n)
=
\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^S
\frac{\sum_g D_{g,s}Z_{g,s}^R}{\sum_g Z_{g,s}^R},
\]
and empirical FDR is the per-replicate fraction of false discoveries among all
discoveries, averaged across $S$ replicates. Because parameters are drawn from
the full pilot distributions $\hat{F}_\mu$, $\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$, and $\hat{F}_\phi$
(Step~1), the power estimate integrates over the observed transcriptome-wide
heterogeneity in baseline expression, amplitude, noise, and phase rather than
relying on a single representative effect size. Because BH
correction is applied within each replicate (Step~3), genome-wide multiplicity
control is captured naturally rather than treated as a post-hoc adjustment.
The indicators $D_{g,s}$ and $Z_{g,s}^R$ introduced here carry over directly
to Section~\ref{sec:differential}, where endpoint-specific superscripts
distinguish among differential endpoints.

A computationally efficient alternative to the full simulation pipeline is also available for the single-cohort setting. Equation~\eqref{eq:circapower_formula}
is evaluated gene-by-gene at each pilot gene's estimated
$\hat{r}_g = \hat{A}_g/\hat{\sigma}_g$ and design factor
$\hat{d}(\hat{\phi}_g)$, and the results are averaged to yield
$\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}_{\mathrm{CF}}(n)$.
This approximation is computationally efficient, but it does not propagate
genome-wide FDR or the joint $(A_g,\sigma_g)$ heterogeneity. The full
simulation-based pipeline is therefore recommended when transcriptome-wide
operating characteristics are the primary design target.

\subsection{Power calculation for case-control differential circadian analysis}
\label{sec:differential}

The single-group indicators $D_{g,s}$ and $Z_{g,s}^R$ from
Section~\ref{sec:single_cohort} generalize to the two-group setting as follows.
For each endpoint $E\in\{\mathrm{DR},\mathrm{DP},\mathrm{DM}\}$,
$D_{g,s}^E\in\{0,1\}$ denotes the endpoint-specific discovery indicator and
$Z_{g,s}^E\in\{0,1\}$ denotes the true differential status. A targeted indicator $Z_{g,s}^{*,E} = Z_{g,s}^E \cdot \mathbf{1}\{\delta_{g,s}^E \geq \Delta_E\}$ restricts attention to genes whose effect size exceeds a user-specified threshold $\Delta_E$, where
$\delta_{g,s}^{\mathrm{DR}} = |R_{g,1,s}^2 - R_{g,2,s}^2|$ is the absolute difference in cosinor goodness-of-fit between groups,
$\delta_{g,s}^{\mathrm{DP}} = \min\!\bigl(|\phi_{g,1,s}-\phi_{g,2,s}|,\,24-|\phi_{g,1,s}-\phi_{g,2,s}|\bigr)$ is the circular phase distance,
and $\delta_{g,s}^{\mathrm{DM}} = |\mu_{g,2,s} - \mu_{g,1,s}|$ is the absolute mesor difference.
Here $R_{g,k,s}^2$ for $k \in \{1, 2\}$ denotes the coefficient of
determination of the cosinor model fit restricted to group $k$, that
is, $R_{g,k,s}^2 = 1 - \mathrm{SSE}_{g,k,s}/\mathrm{SST}_{g,k,s}$,
where $\mathrm{SSE}$ and $\mathrm{SST}$ are the residual and total sums
of squares of the group-$k$ cosinor fit. The group index $k$ used here
is distinct from the harmonic index $k$ in
Section~\ref{sec:cosinor_violation}.

When comparing rhythmic patterns between two groups, inference becomes
multi-stage: genes are first partitioned by per-group rhythmicity, followed by
endpoint-specific tests within data-derived strata. Because the multi-stage inference procedure does not yield tractable analytical
expressions for power and FDR, these quantities are evaluated via simulation
following the full analysis pipeline.

\textsc{SCP} evaluates three endpoints: differential rhythmicity (DR), differential
phase (DP), and differential mesor (DM). DR captures gain or loss of rhythmicity
(e.g., clock genes that cycle in healthy tissue but lose rhythmicity in disease);
DP captures a shift in peak-expression timing; and DM captures a change in
baseline expression level while rhythmic structure is preserved.
Their simulation proportions are denoted
$(\pi_{\mathrm{DR}}, \pi_R, \pi_{\mathrm{DP}}, \pi_{\mathrm{DM}})$.
When pilot data for both groups are available, $\pi_{\mathrm{DR}}$ and
$\pi_{\mathrm{DP}}$ are estimated empirically from the two-group pilot comparison;
$\pi_{\mathrm{DM}}$ is always user-specified, as mesor differences are not reliably
identifiable from the pilot distributions used to calibrate baseline parameters.
Otherwise all proportions are user-specified, with $\pi_R$ initialized from
$\hat{\pi}_R$ by default. When group-specific pilot data are available, the shared
summary $\hat{\Psi}$ can be replaced by group-specific summaries
$\hat{\Psi}_1$ and $\hat{\Psi}_2$, each estimated from the corresponding pilot,
to capture group-level heterogeneity in baseline parameters.
The simulation follows the same four-step structure as
Section~\ref{sec:single_cohort}, with Step~1 shared.

\emph{Step 2: Two-group data generation.}
For each gene, a baseline parameter tuple
$(\mu_g^{(s)}, A_g^{(s)}, \sigma_g^{(s)}, \phi_g^{(s)})$
is sampled from the pilot summary
$\hat{\Psi}=(\hat{F}_{\mu},\hat{F}_{A,\sigma},\hat{F}_{\phi},\hat{F}_{\mathrm{TOD}})$
constructed in Step~1, with $\hat{F}_\mu$ estimated from all $G_0$ genes and
$\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$, $\hat{F}_\phi$ from the rhythmic set $\mathcal{G}_R$.
A latent type $T_g \in \{0,1,2,3,4,5\}$ is then independently assigned
by sampling from a categorical distribution with type probabilities
\[
P(T_g = 1) = \pi_R, \quad
P(T_g = 2) = P(T_g = 3) = \pi_{\mathrm{DR}} / 2, \quad
P(T_g = 4) = \pi_{\mathrm{DP}}, \quad
P(T_g = 5) = \pi_{\mathrm{DM}},
\]
and
$P(T_g = 0) = 1 - \pi_R - \pi_{\mathrm{DR}} - \pi_{\mathrm{DP}} -
\pi_{\mathrm{DM}}$,
where differential rhythmicity is split equally between loss
($T_g = 2$, rhythmic in group 1 only) and gain ($T_g = 3$, rhythmic in
group 2 only).

Group-specific parameters are initialized by setting
$\mu_{g,1}^{(s)} = \mu_{g,2}^{(s)} = \mu_g^{(s)}$,
$A_{g,1}^{(s)} = A_{g,2}^{(s)} = A_g^{(s)}$, and
$\phi_{g,1}^{(s)} = \phi_{g,2}^{(s)} = \phi_g^{(s)}$, and are then modified
according to $T_g$. Observations are generated as
\[
y_{g,i,k}^{(s)} \sim M_{T_g}(\mu_{g,k}^{(s)}, A_{g,k}^{(s)}, \sigma_g^{(s)}, \phi_{g,k}^{(s)}),
\quad k\in\{1,2\},
\]
where $M_1$ denotes the cosinor model and $M_0$ the intercept-only model,
with Gaussian noise $\varepsilon_{g,i,k}^{(s)} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,(\sigma_g^{(s)})^2)$.

Specifically, $T_g=0$ yields arrhythmic genes with $A_{g,1}=A_{g,2}=0$ and
$y_{g,i,k}^{(s)}=\mu_g^{(s)}+\varepsilon_{g,i,k}^{(s)}$ (model $M_0$),
whereas $T_g=1$ yields jointly rhythmic genes with shared parameters across
groups following $M_1$. For differential rhythmicity, $T_g=2$ sets $A_{g,2}=0$
and $T_g=3$ sets $A_{g,1}=0$, so that one group follows $M_1$ and the other
$M_0$. For differential phase ($T_g=4$), both groups follow $M_1$ with
$\phi_{g,2} = (\phi_{g,1} + \Delta \phi_g)\bmod 24$ and
$\Delta \phi_g \sim \mathrm{Unif}(\Delta \phi_{\min}, \Delta \phi_{\max})$;
for differential mesor ($T_g=5$), both groups follow $M_1$ with
$\mu_{g,2} = \mu_{g,1} + \Delta \mu_g$ and
$\Delta \mu_g \sim \mathrm{Unif}(\Delta \mu_{\min}, \Delta \mu_{\max})$.

\emph{Step 3: DCP inference.}
Inference proceeds via \textsc{DiffCircaPipeline}. For each group $k$,
cosinor models are fit and $p$-values $p_{g,k}$ are obtained. Genes are
assigned Test-of-Joint-Rhythmicity (TOJR) labels
\citep{xue2023diffcircapipeline}:
\[
\hat{Z}_g =
\begin{cases}
\texttt{arrhy}   & p_{g,1}>\alpha \text{ and } p_{g,2}>\alpha,\\
\texttt{g1-only} & p_{g,1}\leq\alpha \text{ and } p_{g,2}>\alpha,\\
\texttt{g2-only} & p_{g,1}>\alpha \text{ and } p_{g,2}\leq\alpha,\\
\texttt{both}    & p_{g,1}\leq\alpha \text{ and } p_{g,2}\leq\alpha.
\end{cases}
\]
where $\alpha$ denotes the inference-stage threshold, distinct from the
pilot-stage threshold $\alpha_{\text{pilot}}$ used in Step~1. Endpoint-specific
tests are then applied within strata, followed by BH correction at level $\alpha$.

\emph{Step 4: Power and FDR summarization.}
For each endpoint $E$, let $R_s^E=\sum_g D_{g,s}^E$ and
$V_s^E=\sum_g D_{g,s}^E(1-Z_{g,s}^E)$. The per-replicate FDR is
$\widehat{\mathrm{FDR}}_s^E = V_s^E/R_s^E$ when $R_s^E>0$, and 0 otherwise.
Targeted power is
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{TPower}}_s^E(\Delta_E)
=
\frac{\sum_g D_{g,s}^E Z_{g,s}^{*,E}}{\sum_g Z_{g,s}^{*,E}},
\label{eq:tpower_def}
\end{equation}
where the dependence on $\Delta_E$ enters through the targeted
indicator $Z_{g,s}^{*,E} = Z_{g,s}^E \cdot \mathbf{1}\{\delta_{g,s}^E
\geq \Delta_E\}$ defined above. Final summaries are obtained by
averaging across simulation replicates.

\subsection{Bootstrap uncertainty in projected power}
\label{sec:bootstrap}

The pipeline of Section~\ref{sec:single_cohort} is a two-stage plug-in
procedure: the pilot summary $\hat{\Psi}$ is estimated once and the projected
power $\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}(n)$ is computed conditional on $\hat{\Psi}$.
This treats the pilot as if its parameters were known, and therefore
underrepresents uncertainty in the design recommendation when the pilot
itself is small or weakly informative. In small clinical cohorts the
amplitude--noise quantiles $\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$ and the rhythmic-proportion
estimate $\hat{\pi}_R$ both carry substantial sampling variability, and the
plug-in curve can be a poor representation of the achievable power had a
different pilot been drawn from the same population.

\textsc{SCP} addresses this through an outer nonparametric bootstrap
\citep{efron1979bootstrap} over subjects. For each outer replicate $b = 1, \ldots, B_{\mathrm{out}}$:
\emph{(i)} resample $n_{\mathrm{pilot}}$ subjects with replacement from the
pilot; \emph{(ii)} re-fit the cosinor model and recompute the pilot summary
$\hat{\Psi}^{(b)}$ on the bootstrap pilot; \emph{(iii)} run $N_{\mathrm{sim}}^{\mathrm{in}}$
inner simulations through the Step~2--Step~4 pipeline to obtain
$\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}^{(b)}(n)$. The bootstrap mean and pointwise quantiles
\[
\bar{\mathrm{Power}}(n) = \frac{1}{B_{\mathrm{out}}} \sum_{b=1}^{B_{\mathrm{out}}} \widehat{\mathrm{Power}}^{(b)}(n),
\qquad
\big[\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}^{(b)}(n)\big]_{(b)\,2.5\%}^{(b)\,97.5\%},
\]
furnish a bootstrap-corrected projected power curve and a 95\% confidence
band that reflects the joint variability of $(\hat{F}_{A,\sigma},
\hat{F}_\phi, \hat{\pi}_R)$. The procedure is by construction nested: outer
draws resample subjects, inner draws resample genes from the pilot-conditional
parameter distributions, and the two layers are kept distinct so the
gene-level Monte Carlo error is not confounded with pilot variability.

We illustrate the procedure on two pilots that span more than an
order-of-magnitude in $n_{\mathrm{pilot}}$
(Figure~\ref{fig:bootstrap_sc}): Putamen schizophrenia controls
(GSE160521; $n_{\mathrm{pilot}} = 28$, passive) and \textsc{GTEx}
Thyroid ($n_{\mathrm{pilot}} = 416$, passive). Both panels use
$B_{\mathrm{out}} = 50$ outer draws,
$N_{\mathrm{sim}}^{\mathrm{in}} = 25$ inner simulations, and
$G = 5{,}000$ simulated genes. The widest $95\%$ bootstrap CI is on
Putamen-SCZ at $N = 40$, spanning $[33.5\%, 54.0\%]$; on \textsc{GTEx}
Thyroid the widest $95\%$ CI at $N = 120$ is $[51.4\%, 57.8\%]$,
narrower by roughly four-fold despite the higher elbow power. On
Putamen-SCZ the plug-in projected power at $N = 40$ is $28\%$,
whereas the bootstrap mean is $43\%$; different draws of a
$28$-subject pilot from this population would therefore support
different design recommendations, a gap that the plug-in curve hides.
The contrast between the two pilots shows that the bootstrap CI
tightens with $n_{\mathrm{pilot}}$ until it saturates against the
inner-Monte-Carlo noise floor at large pilots; for
$n_{\mathrm{pilot}} \gtrsim 250$ the bootstrap CI is dominated by the
inner-Monte-Carlo noise rather than pilot variability, and further
increases in $n_{\mathrm{pilot}}$ yield diminishing returns.

A word on interpretation is in order. The bootstrap quantifies
pilot-induced sampling variability of the design recommendation but
does not by itself correct plug-in bias: at $n_{\mathrm{pilot}} = 28$
the cosinor pre-screen at $\alpha_{\mathrm{pilot}} = 0.01$ retains only
the highest-$R^2$ rhythmic genes (a finite-sample winner's-curse effect
on $\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$), so neither the plug-in
$\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}$ nor the bootstrap mean
$\bar{\mathrm{Power}}$ is unbiased for the true population power.
What the bootstrap does provide, and what the plug-in alone hides, is
the magnitude of that uncertainty: at Putamen-SCZ $N = 40$ the $95\%$
bootstrap CI of $33$ to $54\%$ would lead a researcher to question
whether $N = 40$ is adequate, a conclusion the $28\%$ plug-in point
estimate alone could not support either way. The bootstrap is
therefore essential whenever $n_{\mathrm{pilot}}$ is small
($\lesssim 40$); for large, well-rhythmic pilots
($n_{\mathrm{pilot}} \gtrsim 250$) the plug-in recommendation is a
faithful summary.

\subsection{Sensitivity to cosinor violation and the K-harmonic F-test}
\label{sec:cosinor_violation}

The cosinor model \eqref{eq:cosinor_model} assumes a pure sinusoidal
waveform. Real circadian gene expression profiles are frequently asymmetric
or sharply peaked, and the cosinor projection then misses harmonic content
that a flexible detector could in principle recover. This section quantifies
the loss, introduces the K-harmonic F-test that
recovers it, and reports a sensitivity sweep over the waveform-shape and
phase-dispersion parameters of the data-generating process.

Across the six baboon (GSE98965, \citep{Mure2018baboon}) active-design tissues, FMM
\citep{rueda2019fmm} pilot fits yield $\hat{\omega}$ medians from $0.32$
(KIC) to $0.57$ (LIV), far below the cosinor reference $\omega = 1$
(Supplementary Figure~S1). The FMM goodness-of-fit median $R^2$ is
$0.82$--$0.93$ on these tissues, while cosinor $R^2$ on the same genes
is typically lower. Cosinor violation is therefore a typical feature of
high-resolution active-design data, not a hypothetical concern.

Among the parametric circadian families, FMM provides a tractable departure
from sinusoidality. The FMM signal is
\begin{equation}
y(t) = \mu + A\cos\!\bigl(\beta + 2\arctan\!\bigl(\omega\tan\tfrac{t-\alpha}{2}\bigr)\bigr) + \varepsilon,
\qquad \omega \in (0, 1],\;\; \beta \in [0, 2\pi),
\label{eq:fmm_model}
\end{equation}
with shape parameter $\omega$ and orientation parameter $\beta$;
$\alpha$ is the peak location in radians (distinct from the
inference-stage threshold $\alpha$ used in Sections~\ref{sec:single_cohort}
and~\ref{sec:differential}; from here through Section~\ref{sec:active_passive},
$\alpha$ refers to the FMM peak phase), and $\mu, A, \sigma$ retain their
cosinor meaning. At $\omega = 1$ the phase transformation
$u(t) = 2\arctan(\tan((t-\alpha)/2))$ is the identity and
\eqref{eq:fmm_model} collapses to the cosinor model with acrophase
$\phi = \alpha - \beta$; decreasing $\omega$ sharpens and asymmetrises
the waveform along a single interpretable axis.

A direct computation (Supplementary Section~S2;
the M\"{o}bius / Blaschke form $e^{iu(t)} = (e^{is}-\rho)/(1-\rho\,e^{is})$ with
$\rho = (\omega - 1)/(\omega + 1)$ and $s = t - \alpha$) shows that the FMM
signal admits the exact Fourier expansion
\begin{equation}
y(t) = \mu' + A(1 - \rho^2) \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} \rho^{k-1} \cos\!\bigl(k(t - \alpha) + \beta\bigr) + \varepsilon,
\qquad \mu' \equiv \mu - A \rho\cos\beta.
\label{eq:fmm_fourier}
\end{equation}
The $k$th harmonic amplitude is $A(1 - \rho^2)\,|\rho|^{k-1}$ and the geometric
decay rate is $|\rho| = (1 - \omega)/(1 + \omega)$. The Möbius parameter $\rho$ is
distinct from the cosinor effect size $r = A/\sigma$ used in
Section~\ref{sec:single_cohort}: $\rho$ encodes the waveform shape, whereas
$r$ encodes signal strength. The first-harmonic amplitude
relative to the cosinor benchmark $A$ is
\begin{equation}
c(\omega) = \frac{2\omega}{1 + \omega^2},
\label{eq:c_omega}
\end{equation}
and the effective signal-to-noise ratio detectable by the cosinor $F$-test
is $r_{\mathrm{eff}} = (A/\sigma)\,c(\omega)$, so the cosinor power under
FMM truth is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Power}_{\mathrm{cosinor}}^{\mathrm{FMM}}(n, r, \omega) =
\mathrm{Power}_{\mathrm{cosinor}}\!\bigl(n,\, r \cdot c(\omega)\bigr).
\label{eq:fmm_attenuated_power}
\end{equation}
Equation~\eqref{eq:fmm_attenuated_power} is exact whenever the cosinor
detector is applied to FMM-distributed data and recovers the cosinor power
at $\omega = 1$, since $c(1) = 1$. The remaining variance,
$1 - c(\omega)^2$, sits in harmonics $k \geq 2$ that the cosinor $F$-test
cannot access.

\begin{proposition}[$\beta$-invariance of cosinor power under FMM truth]
\label{prop:beta_invariance}
Under the FMM model \eqref{eq:fmm_model}, the noncentrality parameter of
the cosinor $F$-test \eqref{eq:rhythm_f_test} depends on $\omega$ through
$c(\omega)$ in \eqref{eq:c_omega} but is independent of $\beta$. Consequently,
$\mathrm{Power}_{\mathrm{cosinor}}^{\mathrm{FMM}}(n, r, \omega)$ does not
depend on $\beta$.
\end{proposition}
\noindent A short proof: at any harmonic $k$, the squared magnitude of the
Fourier coefficient is $|c_k|^2 + |s_k|^2 = A^2(1-\rho^2)^2 \rho^{2(k-1)}$,
independent of $\beta$. The orientation parameter rotates each harmonic's
phase without changing its amplitude, so the noncentrality parameter of
the cosinor $F$-test, which depends only on the squared first-harmonic
amplitude, is invariant in $\beta$.

The natural likelihood-ratio test of $H_0: A = 0$ in the nonlinear
parameterization \eqref{eq:fmm_model} runs into the Davies boundary
problem \citep{Davies1977,Davies1987}: under $H_0$ the parameters
$\{\beta, \alpha, \omega\}$ are unidentifiable (they multiply zero), and
the LRT statistic does not converge to a $\chi^2$ distribution; instead it
converges to the supremum of a Gaussian process over the unidentifiable
nuisance space. The null has no closed form and requires empirical
calibration; in our hands the calibrated test was about $50\times$ slower
than the cosinor $F$-test and inflated critical values relative to nominal
$F$-thresholds (the ``Davies tax''). The K-harmonic linearisation introduced
below avoids the boundary problem entirely.

Truncating \eqref{eq:fmm_fourier} at $K$ harmonics and \emph{releasing} the
geometric-decay constraint, that is, letting the harmonic coefficients
$(a_k, b_k)$ be free, yields the linear regression
\begin{equation}
y(t) = \mu + \sum_{k=1}^{K} \big[ a_k \cos(k\omega_0 t) + b_k \sin(k\omega_0 t) \big] + \varepsilon,
\qquad \omega_0 = \frac{2\pi}{24},
\label{eq:Kharmonic_model}
\end{equation}
with $2K + 1$ parameters $(\mu, a_1, b_1, \ldots, a_K, b_K)$. The
rhythmicity null is
\[
H_0: a_1 = b_1 = \cdots = a_K = b_K = 0,
\]
and is tested by the standard nested $F$-statistic
\begin{equation}
F_K =
\frac{(\mathrm{SSE}_0 - \mathrm{SSE}_K)/(2K)}{\mathrm{SSE}_K / (n - 2K - 1)},
\qquad
F_K \mid H_0 \;\sim\; F(2K,\, n - 2K - 1)
\quad\text{(exact, finite-sample)},
\label{eq:Kharmonic_F}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{SSE}_0$ and $\mathrm{SSE}_K$ are the residual sums of squares
under the intercept-only and full $K$-harmonic fits. Because
\eqref{eq:Kharmonic_model} is linear in $(\mu, a_k, b_k)$ and all parameters
are identifiable under both $H_0$ and $H_1$, the F-distribution applies
exactly under Gaussianity, with no Davies tax and no empirical calibration.
At $K = 1$ the test reduces to the cosinor $F$-test
\eqref{eq:rhythm_f_test} (DCP). The full derivation, including the
M\"{o}bius reparameterisation, geometric Fourier expansion, and
variance-retention bound $V_K / V_\infty = 1 - \rho^{2K}$, is given in
Supplementary Section~S2.

The K-harmonic regression \eqref{eq:Kharmonic_model} is fitted in
practice using the \textsc{LimoRhyde} framework
\citep{Singer2019LimoRhyde}, which introduced a linear-modelling
pipeline for circadian transcriptomics built on empirical-Bayes
moderated linear regression. \textsc{LimoRhyde} natively supports
$K = 1$ via a cosinor basis with empirical-Bayes variance shrinkage
\citep{Smyth2004limma} and exact $F$-tests on the harmonic block. We
extend this to user-tunable $K$ by stacking per-harmonic bases at
period $T / k$ for $k = 1, \ldots, K$, fitting once via empirical-Bayes
moderated regression, and testing
$H_0: a_1 = b_1 = \cdots = a_K = b_K = 0$ on the joint harmonic block.
Empirical-Bayes shrinkage pools residual variance across the $G$ genes
in the design, which sharpens inference on rhythmicity at moderate
$G$; at small $G$ ($G < 50$) the detector falls back to the unshrunk
$F$-test of \eqref{eq:Kharmonic_F}. The noncentrality formula
\eqref{eq:ncp_K_fmm} continues to apply, because the variance
shrinkage modifies only the estimator and not the underlying linear
model.

The K-harmonic linear model \eqref{eq:Kharmonic_model} has $2K + 1$ free
parameters (one intercept, $K$ cosine coefficients, $K$ sine
coefficients), so its design matrix has $2K + 1$ columns. Identifiability
of the OLS estimator requires the design matrix to have full column
rank, which in turn requires at least $2K + 1$ distinct sampling phases
per period $T$. Below this threshold the regression is rank-deficient,
at least one harmonic coefficient is unestimable, and the $F$-statistic
in \eqref{eq:Kharmonic_F} is conservative or undefined. The same bound
coincides with the Nyquist--Shannon sampling criterion
\citep{Nyquist1928,Shannon1949} applied to trigonometric regression and
has been advocated specifically for circadian transcriptomics
\citep{Hughes2017guidelines}. For the default $K = 2$ the design must
satisfy $B \geq 5$; the K-harmonic detector emits a warning at
dispatch time when this condition is violated. In passive designs the
per-subject collection times are typically distinct, so the condition
is satisfied automatically; in active designs it imposes an explicit
constraint on the sampling grid.

The variance fraction captured by truncation at $K$ is
$V_K / V_\infty = 1 - \rho^{2K}$ where $\rho = (\omega - 1)/(\omega + 1)$,
so capturing more of the non-cosinor variance requires higher $K$.
Each additional harmonic, however, raises the identifiability
threshold to $B \geq 2K + 1$, so denser sampling is needed to support
each step in $K$. At the empirically observed $\hat\omega \in
[0.28, 0.53]$ (Supplementary Figure~S1),
$K = 2$ already captures $90$--$99\%$ of FMM variance, and the marginal
gain at $K = 3$ is at most $7\%$ but requires $B \geq 7$, which most
active circadian designs ($B = 6$ or $8$) do not satisfy. We therefore
adopt $K = 2$ as the default: it covers the dominant non-cosinor
harmonic, is identifiable on any active design with $B \geq 5$, and
has a biological interpretation (12-hour ultradian rhythms are well
documented in liver, gut, and immune tissues). The harmonic order
$K$ is user-tunable.

To characterise the operating region of the $K = 2$ detector on a
cosinor-violating pilot we use a two-axis parametric sweep on the
Baboon LUN pilot ($\hat{\eta} = 1.80$ under the empirical-Bayes
pre-screen, $\hat{\sigma}_\alpha \approx 2.15$\,h, median FMM $R^2 = 0.84$,
active $B = 12$). Per-gene shape and peak parameters are drawn from the
one-parameter families
\[
\omega_g \sim \mathrm{Beta}(1, \eta), \qquad
\alpha_g \sim \mathrm{vonMises}(0, \kappa),\quad
\kappa = (24/(2\pi\sigma_\alpha))^2,
\]
where the second Beta shape $\eta$ is the cross-gene concentration
parameter for $\omega$ (distinct from the per-gene FMM orientation
$\beta$). As $\eta \to 0$ the $\omega$-distribution concentrates at
one (pure cosinor), $\eta = 1$ is uniform on $(0, 1)$, and $\eta \to
\infty$ pushes mass toward zero (the sharp-peak limit). At $N = 144$,
power decreases monotonically with $\eta$:
$\mathrm{Power}(\eta = 0.5) = 0.97$, $\mathrm{Power}(\eta = 1) = 0.95$,
$\mathrm{Power}(\eta = 5) = 0.75$, $\mathrm{Power}(\eta = 20) = 0.35$
(Figure~\ref{fig:fmm_sensitivity}A). The empirical anchor $\hat{\eta} = 1.80$
sits in the high-power region of the curve. The $\alpha$ sweep
($\sigma_\alpha \in \{0, 0.5, 1, 2, 4\}$\,h) is essentially flat at $0.91$
to $0.99$ across all values (Figure~\ref{fig:fmm_sensitivity}B), as
predicted by the cosinor-truth analogue of $\beta$-invariance: at the
genome scale, dispersing peak times rotates harmonics but does not change
their squared amplitudes, so $\mathrm{Power}_{K = 2}$ is approximately
invariant in $\sigma_\alpha$ at fixed waveform shape.

\subsection{Active vs passive design}
\label{sec:active_passive}

The two preceding sections motivate two detectors, the cosinor $F$-test
(DCP, $K = 1$) and the K-harmonic F-test (default $K = 2$), and the
question of which design is preferable interacts with
which detector is in use. We resolve this interaction in two steps: first
a brief noncentrality calculation under cosinor and FMM truth, then an
empirical comparison on one active and one passive pilot.

Under cosinor truth and a balanced equally spaced active design with
$B \geq 3$, the design factor $d(\phi) = 1/2$ uniformly in $\phi$
(Section~\ref{sec:single_cohort}), and the DCP noncentrality is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{NCP}_{\mathrm{DCP}}^{\mathrm{cos}}(N) = \frac{(A/\sigma)^2\, N}{2},
\label{eq:ncp_dcp_cos}
\end{equation}
which is invariant in the $(B, m)$ allocation at fixed $N$. Under FMM
truth, by \eqref{eq:fmm_attenuated_power},
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{NCP}_{\mathrm{DCP}}^{\mathrm{FMM}}(N, \omega) = \frac{(A \cdot c(\omega)/\sigma)^2\, N}{2},
\label{eq:ncp_dcp_fmm}
\end{equation}
which is still $(B, m)$-invariant in design but attenuated by
$c(\omega) < 1$ per gene. The variance lost relative to the cosinor benchmark
is $1 - c(\omega)^2$, and that variance lives in harmonics $k \geq 2$. The
K-harmonic F-test recovers it whenever $B \geq 2K + 1$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{NCP}_{\mathrm{K}}^{\mathrm{FMM}}(N) \approx \frac{N}{2\sigma^2} \sum_{k=1}^{K} |c_k|^2,
\label{eq:ncp_K_fmm}
\end{equation}
with $|c_k|^2 = A^2(1 - \rho^2)^2 \rho^{2(k-1)}$ from \eqref{eq:fmm_fourier};
when $B < 2K + 1$ the regression \eqref{eq:Kharmonic_model} is rank-deficient
and \eqref{eq:ncp_K_fmm} is not realised. \eqref{eq:ncp_dcp_cos}--\eqref{eq:ncp_K_fmm}
make two predictions: DCP power should be approximately $B$-invariant under
both truths, and the K-harmonic F-test should dominate DCP when
$c(\omega) < 1$ and the Nyquist condition is met.

We test these predictions empirically in
Section~\ref{sec:active_passive_results} on the active baboon KIM
pilot, using Figure~\ref{fig:active_vs_passive}.

The picture that emerges is not ``active wins'' or ``passive wins'' in
any absolute sense. Within active designs, neither DCP nor the K-harmonic
F-test depends on the $(B, m)$ allocation at fixed $N$ above its
identifiability threshold, so the $B$-versus-$m$ trade-off classically
emphasised in design guidance \citep{Hughes2017guidelines} reduces to a
single choice: pick any $B \geq 3$ (DCP) or $B \geq 5$ ($K = 2$), then
allocate $N = B \times m$ subjects in whatever way is logistically
convenient. The detector choice is governed by the pilot waveform rather
than by the active vs. passive distinction: we recommend the K-harmonic
F-test ($K = 2$) when the pilot $\hat{\omega}$-median is $\leq 0.6$ and
the design satisfies $B \geq 2K + 1$, and the cosinor $F$-test otherwise.
In our pilots, baboon active tissues fall in the K-harmonic regime and
human passive cohorts fall in the cosinor regime, but the underlying
criterion is waveform shape rather than design type. The two detectors
share the same upstream parameter estimation and downstream BH pipeline,
so the choice does not affect the rest of the framework.

Single-cohort power is driven by the $\tilde{r}$ distribution rather than any
single summary statistic (Figure~\ref{fig:sc_adrliv}). For \textsc{GTEx}

strata ($\tilde{r}>2$) saturate at $N\approx 20$--$30$, whereas genes with
$\tilde{r}<0.5$ remain substantially underpowered even at $N=200$. The broad
power range across strata within a single dataset shows why pilot-data-driven
simulation, rather than a single effect size, is essential for realistic study
planning.

\section{Discussion}
%% ====================================================================

The pilot datasets in our test set span roughly a two-fold range in
passive-design $\tilde{r}$ (from $0.45$ for \textsc{GTEx} Thyroid to
$1.02$ for the Putamen control cohort), and the resulting $N_{80}$
estimates differ by more than a factor of four. At \textsc{GTEx}
Adrenal Gland ($\tilde{r}\approx 0.97$, passive),
$N_{80} \approx 49$; at \textsc{GTEx} Liver
($\tilde{r} \approx 0.48$, passive), $N_{80}$ exceeds the $N \leq 200$
simulation grid. This reproduces the core message of \textsc{PROPER}
for RNA-seq \citep{wu2015proper} in the circadian setting and
reinforces the need for effect-size-aware, dataset-specific planning
rather than generic ``circadian sample size'' recommendations.

The equispaced active design achieves a noncentrality parameter
$\lambda_g = N r_g^2 / 2$, twice the value of an unstructured passive
design with the same $N$ (design factor $d = 1/2$ vs.\ $d \approx 0.24$
for typical post-mortem TOD distributions). The active baboon pilots
in Figures~\ref{fig:fmm_sensitivity} and~\ref{fig:active_vs_passive}
(LUN $\tilde{r} \approx 1.72$, KIM $\hat\eta = 2.62$) illustrate this
regime empirically: on KIM the $K = 2$ harmonic test reaches $80\%$
power at $N \approx 50$ across all sampling grids
$B \in \{6, 8, 12, 24\}$. Passive designs sacrifice this efficiency but
remain the only option for human post-mortem brain, clinical-cohort
chronobiology, and any setting where collection time is determined by
external scheduling rather than experimental protocol; \textsc{SCP}
supports both modes with the same parameter-estimation and inference
machinery.

Detector choice and time-sampling allocation decouple cleanly. The
K-harmonic F-test (default $K = 2$) is the right detector when the
pilot shows non-cosinor waveforms, operationalised through the pilot
$\hat{\omega}$-median: empirically, the baboon active tissues have
$\hat{\omega}$-median in $[0.28, 0.53]$ (Supplementary Figure~S1).
We recommend
$K = 2$ when the pilot $\hat{\omega}$-median is $\leq 0.6$ and the design
satisfies $B \geq 2K + 1 = 5$, and DCP (which is the K-harmonic F-test at
$K = 1$) otherwise. The general harmonic-regression identifiability rule
is $B \geq 2K + 1$ distinct sampling phases per period: $B \geq 3$ for
DCP, $B \geq 5$ for $K = 2$, $B \geq 7$ for $K = 3$. Above this
threshold the $(B, m)$ allocation does not affect power at fixed $N$: on the
active KIM pilot, the four sampling grids $B \in \{6, 8, 12, 24\}$
overlap within Monte Carlo noise ($\leq 4$\,\%) for both detectors
(Figure~\ref{fig:active_vs_passive}). The $B$-versus-$m$ trade-off
classically emphasised in design guidance therefore reduces, once the
identifiability bound is met, to a single choice of total $N$. The
\textsc{SCP} detector slot is generic and accepts any
$p$-value-emitting rhythmicity test, so the framework extends to
\textsc{JTK}, \textsc{RAIN}, and other published methods by drop-in
replacement; this paper focuses on DCP and the K-harmonic F-test because
they have closed-form noncentralities that make planning recommendations
analytically tractable.

The bootstrap of Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap} surfaces pilot-induced
uncertainty in the design recommendation but does not by itself correct
plug-in bias. The two panels of Figure~\ref{fig:bootstrap_sc} span
$n_{\mathrm{pilot}} \in \{28, 416\}$ with widest $95\%$ CIs of
$[33.5\%, 54.0\%]$ (Putamen-SCZ, $n_{\mathrm{pilot}} = 28$, at
$N = 40$) and $[51.4\%, 57.8\%]$ (\textsc{GTEx} Thyroid,
$n_{\mathrm{pilot}} = 416$, at $N = 120$); the CI saturates against
the inner-Monte-Carlo noise floor at the large-pilot end. At
Putamen-SCZ ($n_{\mathrm{pilot}} = 28$) the bootstrap is decisive:
the plug-in projects $28\%$ power at $N = 40$ but the $95\%$ CI of
$33$ to $54\%$ would lead a researcher to question whether $N = 40$ is
adequate, a conclusion the plug-in point estimate cannot support
either way. For intermediate pilots
($40 < n_{\mathrm{pilot}} < 250$) the bootstrap serves as a useful
sanity check on the plug-in projection; for large pilots
($n_{\mathrm{pilot}} \gtrsim 250$) the plug-in recommendation is
faithful.

DR, DP, and DM tests are conditional on a data-derived TOJR
classification, which breaks analytic tractability and increases the
required sample size per group relative to single-cohort detection.
On the passive \textsc{GTEx} Adrenal Gland vs.\ Liver comparison
(Figure~\ref{fig:diff_adrliv}), the per-group $N_{80}$ at FDR $0.20$
is approximately $120$ for DP, $190$ for DR, and $190$ for DM. The
endpoint ordering reflects effect-size structure rather than
detector behaviour: DP benefits from the most concentrated
phase-shift effect-size distribution in this comparison, while DR
and DM are comparably hard, with DM marginally weaker because the
mesor-shift signal is the smallest of the three. The two-stage cost
is roughly $2$ to $5\times$ the matched single-cohort $N_{80}$, with
the gap widest at low $\tilde{r}$ where TOJR classification is
noisiest.

\subsection{A decision tree for circadian study planning}
\label{sec:decision_tree}

We close with a four-step rule for using \textsc{SCP} prospectively.

\textbf{Step 1, detector.}
From a pilot dataset, estimate $\hat{\omega}$-median over the rhythmic
gene set (Section~\ref{sec:cosinor_violation}). If $\hat{\omega}$-median
is $\leq 0.6$ and the planned design will satisfy $B \geq 5$, use the
$K = 2$ harmonic F-test; otherwise use the
cosinor F-test (DCP). The choice between the two is governed by
waveform shape, not by active vs.\ passive design \emph{per se}.

\textbf{Step 2, sampling grid.}
Choose any $B$ at or above the harmonic-regression identifiability
threshold $B \geq 2K + 1$: that is, $B \geq 3$ for DCP, $B \geq 5$ for
$K = 2$, $B \geq 7$ for $K = 3$. Above the threshold, the $(B, m)$
allocation does not affect power; the design choice reduces to a
single decision on total $N$. The detection functions
Both detectors are exposed as stand-alone functions in the framework
so that they can be applied outside the power-analysis pipeline, with
the same identifiability requirement on the supplied design.

\textbf{Step 3, sample size.}
Read $N_{80}$ off the closed-form power curve at your pilot
$\tilde{r}$-median (Sections~\ref{sec:single_cohort}
and~\ref{sec:differential}); typical single-cohort operating points
observed in our pilots are $N_{80} \lesssim 50$ for active
moderate- to high-$\tilde{r}$ tissue (e.g., baboon active pilots,
$\tilde{r} \geq 1.5$), $N_{80} \approx 50$ for passive
moderately-rhythmic tissue ($\tilde{r} \approx 1.0$), and
$N_{80} > 200$ for passive low-$\tilde{r}$ tissue
($\tilde{r} \lesssim 0.5$). For differential
analyses, plan per group: on the passive \textsc{GTEx} Adrenal Gland
vs.\ Liver comparison, FDR-$0.20$ per-group $N_{80}$ is approximately
$120$ for DP, $190$ for DR, and $190$ for DM.

\textbf{Step 4, bootstrap.}
If $n_{\mathrm{pilot}} \leq 40$, run the outer subject-bootstrap of
Section~\ref{sec:bootstrap} and report the $95\%$ CI band alongside
the plug-in curve; the bootstrap is essential at this pilot size to
surface the uncertainty the plug-in alone hides. For intermediate
pilots ($40 < n_{\mathrm{pilot}} < 250$) the bootstrap CI serves as
a useful sanity check on the plug-in projection. For
$n_{\mathrm{pilot}} \gtrsim 250$, the plug-in curve is a faithful
summary and the bootstrap is optional.

The cosinor model assumes a single sinusoidal harmonic and the
K-harmonic F-test assumes Gaussian residuals; both are robust to mild
violations but degrade for heavily sharp-peaked, near-arrhythmic, or
heteroskedastic genes. Gene types in the differential simulator are
modelled as mutually exclusive, so compound DR+DP changes are not
directly evaluated. Passive-design power depends on the empirical TOD
distribution of the pilot, which may not generalise to a planned
cohort with different recruitment logistics. The bootstrap is
computationally intensive ($B_{\mathrm{out}} \times
N_{\mathrm{sim}}^{\mathrm{in}}$ inner simulations) and is
recommended only where the analysis above indicates pilot uncertainty
is large enough to matter.

\textsc{SCP} is available as an open-source R framework.
