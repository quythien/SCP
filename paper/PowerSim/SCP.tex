\begin{abstract}
Circadian disruption is a hallmark of many human diseases, yet detecting condition-specific changes in gene rhythms remains challenging. Circadian transcriptomic studies aim to identify rhythmic genes within a condition or characterize differences across conditions, including differential rhythmicity and phase shifts, but prospective study design remains underdeveloped. Existing approaches (e.g., \textsc{CircaPower}) provide analytical power calculations for single-condition studies under a fixed signal-to-noise ratio $r = A/\sigma$, but do not extend to differential analysis or account for gene-level heterogeneity.

We present \textsc{SCP} (Simulation-based Circadian Power), a unified framework for sample-size planning in circadian transcriptomic studies. In the single-condition setting, \textsc{SCP} extends closed-form circadian power calculations with pilot-data-driven transcriptome-wide simulation; in the differential setting, it uses pilot-data-driven simulation to evaluate power and false discovery rate (FDR) under both active and passive designs. A bootstrap procedure quantifies uncertainty in power curves and sample-size recommendations.

Applied to human prefrontal cortex data (19{,}998 genes; $n = 62$), power to detect differential rhythmicity remains limited (30.5\% at $n = 100$ per group, FDR 5\%), with substantial variation across genes. \textsc{SCP} enables principled design of circadian transcriptomic studies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Circadian rhythms, endogenous $\sim$24-hour oscillations, play a fundamental role in physiology and disease \citep{takahashi2017transcriptional}. Disruption of circadian timing has been implicated in aging and neuropsychiatric disorders \citep{chen2016aging}, and rhythmic transcription regulates metabolism, synaptic function, and immune signaling \citep{takahashi2017transcriptional,mure2018diurnal,chen2016aging}. To unveil circadian regulation in transcriptomic data, studies generally pursue one of two goals: detecting potential circadian biomarkers within a single condition or characterizing differences in rhythmic patterns across conditions, including differential rhythmicity (DR), differential phase (DP), and differential mesor (DM). A growing number of methods have been developed for both settings, including rhythmicity detection methods such as \textsc{JTK\_CYCLE}, \textsc{RAIN}, and \textsc{BIO\_CYCLE} for single-condition studies \citep{Hughes2010jtkcycle,Thaben2014rain,Agostinelli2016biocycle}, and differential frameworks such as \textsc{CircaCompare}, \textsc{LimoRhyde}, and \textsc{DiffCircaPipeline} for cross-condition analysis \citep{parsons2020circacompare,Singer2019LimoRhyde,xue2023diffcircapipeline}. While these methods enable statistical inference on circadian signals, they do not address how studies should be designed to achieve reliable detection.

Prospective study design therefore remains a major bottleneck. Investigators must determine the sample size required to achieve adequate power, assess how sensitive those recommendations are to limited pilot data, and, in active designs, balance temporal resolution against replication. These challenges are especially pronounced in passive observational studies, such as post-mortem human brain experiments, where sampling times cannot be controlled and each additional sample is costly \citep{zong2023circapower}. Without principled design guidance, circadian transcriptomic studies risk being underpowered despite substantial experimental investment.

To our knowledge, \textsc{CircaPower} is currently the only explicit framework for power and sample-size determination in circadian transcriptomic studies, and it is restricted to single-condition rhythmicity detection. It derives closed-form power calculations under a cosinor model using a fixed intrinsic effect size \( r = A/\sigma \), thereby quantifying per-gene statistical power for detecting rhythmicity. While this provides an important foundation for circadian biomarker discovery, the formulation is inherently gene-level and primarily addresses false negatives through power. However, empirical evidence indicates that \( r \) varies substantially across tissues, species, and gene classes, with marked differences between canonical clock genes and the broader transcriptome \citep{zong2023circapower}. Consequently, the assumption of a fixed effect size fails to capture the heterogeneity in signal strength across genes. Moreover, because circadian studies operate at the transcriptome-wide level, controlling FDR is essential. The fixed-\( r \) formulation does not naturally accommodate this requirement, as it lacks a mechanism to model cross-gene variability needed to characterize genome-wide error rates.

This limitation is especially consequential in transcriptome-wide applications, where study performance depends on the distribution of effect sizes across genes rather than on a single representative signal level. In addition, because thousands of genes are tested simultaneously, design-stage operating characteristics must be evaluated under genome-wide multiple testing rather than at the level of an isolated gene.

These limitations become more pronounced in differential circadian analysis. In this setting, inference is inherently multi-stage and genome-wide: one must evaluate rhythmic structure across groups, test for DR, DP, and DM, and adjust for multiple comparisons. As a result, study performance depends not only on per-gene detection, but also on how these steps interact at the transcriptome level. From a statistical perspective, this multi-stage structure involves multiple endpoints together with genome-wide multiple testing, and therefore cannot be summarized by a single test statistic or simple analytical expression. The likelihood governing the overall procedure does not admit a tractable closed-form expression, making it difficult to derive analytical formulas for power or FDR. Existing differential methods focus on statistical testing and on assigning genes to differential circadian categories, but no analogous framework currently exists for sample-size planning or evaluating design-stage operating characteristics.

These gaps motivate a unified framework for circadian study design that integrates single-condition biomarker detection and multi-condition differential analysis while accounting for gene-level heterogeneity and realistic downstream inference. We introduce \textbf{SCP} (Simulation-based Circadian Power), a general framework for sample-size planning in circadian transcriptomic studies. In the single-condition setting, \textsc{SCP} extends existing analytical power calculations with pilot-data-driven transcriptome-wide simulation, thereby supporting both a fast closed-form approximation and simulation-based evaluation under genome-wide heterogeneity and multiple testing. In the differential setting, where analytical solutions are intractable, \textsc{SCP} uses pilot-data-driven simulation to evaluate transcriptome-level performance under candidate designs, including power and FDR for DR, DP, and DM. Because pilot datasets are often limited in size, \textsc{SCP} also uses bootstrap resampling to quantify uncertainty in estimated power curves and sample-size recommendations. By combining analytical efficiency with simulation-based flexibility, \textsc{SCP} provides researchers with a principled framework for designing circadian transcriptomic studies.

\section{Methods}
%% ====================================================================

\textsc{SCP} (Simulation-based Circadian Power) provides a unified framework
for sample-size planning in circadian transcriptomic studies. For single-cohort
rhythmicity detection, \textsc{SCP} extends the closed-form
\textsc{CircaPower} formula to the transcriptome-wide setting by estimating
gene-specific effect sizes from a pilot dataset and averaging power across the
full transcriptome-wide distribution of signal-to-noise ratios. For
differential circadian analysis, the multi-stage inference pipeline creates
dependence between rhythmicity screening and endpoint testing that cannot be
captured in closed form; simulation therefore provides a natural and necessary
framework for evaluating power and FDR under realistic study conditions.

%% --------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{SCP for Circadian Biomarker Detection (Single Cohort)}
\label{sec:single_cohort}

For a single group of $n$ subjects with collection times
$t_1,\ldots,t_n\in[0,24)$, the expression of gene $g$ follows the cosinor model
\begin{equation}
y_{g,i}
=
\mu_g
+
A_g\cos\!\bigl(\omega(t_i - \phi_g)\bigr)
+
\varepsilon_{g,i},
\qquad
\varepsilon_{g,i}\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim}N(0,\sigma_g^2),
\label{eq:cosinor_model}
\end{equation}
where $\omega=2\pi/24$, $\mu_g$ is the mesor, $A_g\geq 0$ is the amplitude,
$\phi_g\in[0,24)$ is the acrophase, and $\sigma_g$ is the noise level. The
intrinsic effect size is $r_g = A_g/\sigma_g$. Rhythmicity of gene $g$ is
assessed by testing $H_0: A_g = 0$ using the $F$-statistic
\begin{equation}
F_g
=
\frac{(\mathrm{TSS}_g - \mathrm{RSS}_g)/2}{\mathrm{RSS}_g/(n-3)}
\sim F(2,\, n-3),
\label{eq:rhythm_f_test}
\end{equation}
where $\mathrm{RSS}_g$ and $\mathrm{TSS}_g$ are the residual and total sums of
squares from the cosinor fit. Across $G$ genes, Benjamini--Hochberg (BH)
correction at nominal FDR level $\alpha$ controls FDR for
rhythmicity detection.

The noncentrality parameter of the $F$-test depends jointly on the effect size
and the sampling design:
\begin{equation}
\lambda_g = r_g^2 \cdot n \cdot d(\phi_g),
\label{eq:ncp}
\end{equation}
where the design factor $d(\phi_g)$ captures how well the collection times
cover the gene's peak expression time:
\begin{equation}
d(\phi_g)
=
\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \cos^2\!\bigl(\omega(t_i - \phi_g)\bigr)
-
\left(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \cos\!\bigl(\omega(t_i - \phi_g)\bigr)\right)^{\!2}.
\label{eq:design_factor_circapower}
\end{equation}
Per-gene power at sample size $n$ is then
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Power}_g(n)
=
1 - F_{2,\,n-3}\!\bigl(F_{1-\alpha,\,2,\,n-3};\, \lambda_g\bigr),
\label{eq:circapower_formula}
\end{equation}
where $F_{2,n-3}(\cdot\,;\,\lambda_g)$ is the CDF of the non-central $F$
distribution with noncentrality $\lambda_g$ \citep{zong2023circapower}. For
balanced equally spaced active designs with $B\geq 3$ time points,
$d(\phi_g)=1/2$ for all phases (B-invariance; see Supplementary~S8), so
$\lambda_g = r_g^2 \cdot n / 2$ and power depends only on $r_g$ and $n$.

This closed-form approach, as implemented in \textsc{CircaPower}, evaluates
\eqref{eq:circapower_formula} at a single representative $r$ specified by the
user. In transcriptome-wide studies, however, $r_g$ varies substantially across
genes: core clock genes in mouse liver typically have $r \approx 3$--$6$, while
the median $r$ across all expressed genes in human brain tissue is
$\tilde{r}\approx 0.6$ \citep{zong2023circapower}. Using a single high $r$
therefore produces severely over-optimistic power projections for the majority
of the transcriptome, and the per-gene formula \eqref{eq:circapower_formula}
does not account for the multiple-testing correction applied when testing
thousands of genes simultaneously.

\textsc{SCP} addresses these limitations through a four-step simulation
pipeline. A closed-form shortcut is also available: \eqref{eq:circapower_formula}
is evaluated gene-by-gene at each pilot gene's estimated
$\hat{r}_g = \hat{A}_g/\hat{\sigma}_g$ and design factor $\hat{d}(\hat{\phi}_g)$,
and the results are averaged to yield $\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}_{\mathrm{CF}}(n)$.
This approximation is computationally efficient, but it does not propagate
genome-wide FDR or the joint $(A_g,\sigma_g)$ heterogeneity. The full
simulation-based pipeline is therefore recommended when transcriptome-wide
operating characteristics are the primary design target and proceeds as
follows.

\emph{Step 1 --- Pilot parameter estimation.}
For each of the $G_0$ pilot genes, fit \eqref{eq:cosinor_model} by OLS to obtain
$(\hat{\mu}_g, \hat{A}_g, \hat{\sigma}_g, \hat{\phi}_g)$ and a BH-adjusted
rhythmicity $q$-value $\hat{q}_g$; the rhythmic gene set is
$\mathcal{G}_R = \{g : \hat{q}_g < 0.10\}$ and $\hat{\pi}_R = |\mathcal{G}_R|/G_0$.
Empirical distributions are then constructed over the fitted parameters:
\begin{equation}
\hat{F}_\mu
=
\frac{1}{G_0}\sum_{g=1}^{G_0}\delta_{\hat{\mu}_g},
\quad
\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}_R|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_R}\delta_{(\hat{A}_g,\hat{\sigma}_g)},
\quad
\hat{F}_{\phi}
=
\frac{1}{|\mathcal{G}_R|}\sum_{g\in\mathcal{G}_R}\delta_{\hat{\phi}_g}.
\label{eq:empirical_dists}
\end{equation}
Sampling $(A,\sigma)$ jointly from $\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$ preserves the empirical
amplitude-noise dependence, so the induced $r=A/\sigma$ distribution reflects
the full pilot transcriptome without requiring $r$ to be specified separately.
The complete pilot summary $\hat{\Psi} = (\hat{F}_\mu, \hat{F}_{A,\sigma},
\hat{F}_\phi, \hat{F}_{\mathrm{TOD}}, \hat{\pi}_R)$ is passed to the downstream
steps, where $\hat{F}_{\mathrm{TOD}}$ is the empirical collection-time
distribution for passive designs.

\emph{Step 2 --- Data generation.}
For each simulation replicate $s=1,\ldots,S$ and candidate sample size $n$,
draw $G$ gene-level parameter tuples by sampling $\mu_g^{(s)}\sim\hat{F}_\mu$,
$(A_g^{(s)},\sigma_g^{(s)})\sim\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$, and $\phi_g^{(s)}\sim\hat{F}_\phi$,
with rhythmicity status $Z_{g,s}^R\sim\mathrm{Bernoulli}(\hat\pi_R)$. Observations
for rhythmic genes are generated from \eqref{eq:cosinor_model}; arrhythmic genes
have $A_g=0$. An \emph{active} design specifies $B$ distinct time points with
$m_b$ replicates each and total $n=\sum_b m_b$; a \emph{passive} design draws
collection times from $\hat{F}_{\mathrm{TOD}}$, as in post-mortem cohorts where
clinical timing governs sampling. For balanced equally spaced active designs with
$B\geq 3$, $d(\phi)=1/2$ for all phases (Supplementary~S8), so power depends
only on total $n$; for passive designs the realized
$d(\phi_g)$ is computed from the observed collection times via
\eqref{eq:design_factor_circapower}, varies by gene and replicate, and
\textsc{SCP} reports design-factor diagnostics to
quantify how temporal coverage affects efficiency.

\emph{Step 3 --- Inference.}
Apply the $F$-test \eqref{eq:rhythm_f_test} to every gene and BH correction at
nominal level $\alpha$ across all $G$ genes. Let $D_{g,s}\in\{0,1\}$ denote
the discovery indicator ($D_{g,s}=1$ if gene $g$ is declared rhythmic in
replicate $s$).

\emph{Step 4 --- Power aggregation.}
With $Z_{g,s}^R\in\{0,1\}$ the true rhythmicity status assigned in Step~2,
empirical power is
\[
\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}(n)
=
\frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^S
\frac{\sum_g D_{g,s}Z_{g,s}^R}{\sum_g Z_{g,s}^R},
\]
and empirical FDR is the per-replicate fraction of false discoveries among all
discoveries, averaged across $S$ replicates. Because gene parameters are drawn
from the full pilot distribution $\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$ (Step~2), the power
estimate integrates over the complete transcriptome-wide distribution of
signal-to-noise ratios rather than a single representative $r$; because BH
correction is applied within each replicate (Step~3), genome-wide multiplicity
control is captured naturally rather than treated as a post-hoc adjustment.
The notation $D_{g,s}$ and $Z_{g,s}^R$ introduced here extends directly to the
differential setting in Section~\ref{sec:differential}, where endpoint-specific
superscripts are added.

\subsection{Differential Circadian Analysis}
\label{sec:differential}

When the goal is to compare rhythmic patterns between two groups (e.g.,
disease vs.\ control), the inference problem changes fundamentally. The
analysis pipeline is multi-stage: a joint rhythmicity classifier first assigns
each gene to a conditional pool based on per-group $p$-values, and
endpoint-specific tests are then applied within those data-derived strata.
Because the conditional structure of the pipeline cannot be expressed in closed
form, power and FDR cannot be derived analytically even for simple two-group
designs. This motivates a simulation-based framework in which the entire
analysis pipeline is evaluated numerically, making it possible to characterize
study performance under the actual inference procedure and under realistic
parameter settings drawn from pilot data.

\textsc{SCP} evaluates three differential endpoints. Differential rhythmicity (DR) captures gain or loss of rhythmicity: the gene is rhythmic in one group but not the other \citep{Singer2019LimoRhyde,ding2021likelihood,xue2023diffcircapipeline}. Biologically, DR corresponds to clock gene disruption --- for example, core oscillator genes that cycle in healthy tissue but lose rhythmicity in disease states \citep{takahashi2017transcriptional,chen2016aging,seney2019diurnal}. Differential phase (DP) indicates a shifted acrophase, $\phi_{g,1}\neq\phi_{g,2}$, arising when the timing of peak expression changes \citep{parsons2020circacompare,ding2021likelihood}. Differential mesor (DM) captures a shifted baseline expression level, $\mu_{g,1}\neq\mu_{g,2}$, while the rhythmic structure is otherwise preserved \citep{parsons2020circacompare,Singer2019LimoRhyde}. Although these endpoints may co-occur in real data, \textsc{SCP} evaluates them in separate simulation scenarios to produce interpretable, endpoint-specific power curves \citep{zong2023circapower}.

The notation from Section~\ref{sec:single_cohort} extends to the two-group
setting as follows. Groups are indexed by $k\in\{1,2\}$. For each endpoint
$E\in\{\mathrm{DR},\mathrm{DP},\mathrm{DM}\}$, the single-group discovery
indicator $D_{g,s}$ is replaced by an endpoint-specific indicator
$D_{g,s}^E\in\{0,1\}$, and the single-group true-status indicator $Z_{g,s}^R$ is
replaced by $Z_{g,s}^E\in\{0,1\}$ (truly differential for endpoint $E$). To
evaluate power at a minimum biologically relevant effect size, a targeted
indicator
$Z_{g,s}^{*,E} = Z_{g,s}^E\cdot\mathbf{1}\{\delta_{g,s}^E \geq \Delta_E\}$
selects genes exceeding a user-specified endpoint-specific threshold
$\Delta_E$, where $\delta_{g,s}^{\mathrm{DR}}$ is the simulated effect size
defining gain or loss of rhythmicity, $\delta_{g,s}^{\mathrm{DP}}$ is the
absolute phase shift, and $\delta_{g,s}^{\mathrm{DM}}$ is the absolute mesor
difference. The proportions of DR, DP, DM, and jointly rhythmic genes are denoted
$\pi_{\mathrm{DR}}$, $\pi_{\mathrm{DP}}$, $\pi_{\mathrm{DM}}$, and $\pi_R$.

The differential pipeline follows the same four-step structure as
Section~\ref{sec:single_cohort}. Step~1 is shared, whereas Steps~2--4 are
adapted to the two-group setting. In each endpoint-specific simulation
scenario, genes are assigned mutually exclusive latent states so that power for
the target endpoint can be evaluated cleanly without overlap from other
differential alternatives.

\emph{Step 2 --- Two-group data generation.}
For each gene, \textsc{SCP} begins from pilot-derived baseline parameter
distributions. When pilot data are available for both groups, group-specific
pilot summaries
$\hat{\Psi}_k=(\hat{F}_{\mu,k},\hat{F}_{A,\sigma,k},\hat{F}_{\phi,k},
\hat{F}_{\mathrm{TOD},k})$ for $k\in\{1,2\}$ are estimated and baseline
parameters are drawn separately for each group:
\[
\mu_{g,k}^{(s)}\sim\hat{F}_{\mu,k},\qquad
(A_{g,k}^{(s)},\sigma_{g,k}^{(s)})\sim\hat{F}_{A,\sigma,k},\qquad
\phi_{g,k}^{(s)}\sim\hat{F}_{\phi,k}.
\]
When only one pilot group is available, a single reference summary
$\hat{\Psi}=(\hat{F}_{\mu},\hat{F}_{A,\sigma},\hat{F}_{\phi},
\hat{F}_{\mathrm{TOD}})$ is used to draw a baseline tuple
$(\mu_g^{(s)},A_g^{(s)},\sigma_g^{(s)},\phi_g^{(s)})$, with
$\mu_g^{(s)}\sim\hat{F}_{\mu}$,
$(A_g^{(s)},\sigma_g^{(s)})\sim\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$, and
$\phi_g^{(s)}\sim\hat{F}_{\phi}$. This baseline defines group 1, so
$\mu_{g,1}^{(s)}=\mu_g^{(s)}$, $A_{g,1}^{(s)}=A_g^{(s)}$,
$\sigma_{g,1}^{(s)}=\sigma_g^{(s)}$, and $\phi_{g,1}^{(s)}=\phi_g^{(s)}$;
group 2 is then generated by applying the endpoint-specific perturbations
described below. In this one-pilot setting, the two groups share the same
baseline noise level unless a future extension explicitly models
heteroscedasticity.

Each gene is then assigned a latent type $T_g\in\{0,1,2,3,4,5\}$ drawn from
the user-supplied proportions
$(\pi_{\mathrm{DR}},\pi_R,\pi_{\mathrm{DP}},\pi_{\mathrm{DM}})$.
Arrhythmic genes ($T_g=0$, probability
$1-\pi_{\mathrm{DR}}-\pi_R-\pi_{\mathrm{DP}}-\pi_{\mathrm{DM}}$) have
$A_{g,1}=A_{g,2}=0$. Jointly rhythmic genes ($T_g=1$, probability $\pi_R$)
retain their baseline parameters in both groups. DR genes are split equally
between loss of rhythmicity ($T_g=2$, group-1 rhythmic only) and gain of
rhythmicity ($T_g=3$, group-2 rhythmic only), each with probability
$\pi_{\mathrm{DR}}/2$; for $T_g=2$ set $A_{g,2}=0$, and for $T_g=3$ set
$A_{g,1}=0$. In the one-pilot setting, these DR types are generated by
perturbing the shared reference baseline; in the two-pilot setting, they are
imposed relative to the separately sampled group-specific baselines.
Phase-shifted genes ($T_g=4$, probability $\pi_{\mathrm{DP}}$) retain the
same mesor, amplitude, and noise level under the simulated endpoint scenario
but have $\phi_{g,2}=(\phi_{g,1}+\Delta\phi_g)\bmod 24$ with
$\Delta\phi_g\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\Delta\phi_{\min},\Delta\phi_{\max})$.
Differential-mesor genes ($T_g=5$, probability $\pi_{\mathrm{DM}}$) retain the
same amplitude, phase, and noise level under the simulated endpoint scenario
but have
$\mu_{g,2}=\mu_{g,1}+\Delta\mu_g$ with
$\Delta\mu_g\sim\mathrm{Unif}(\Delta\mu_{\min},\Delta\mu_{\max})$.
The full parameter mapping is given in Table~\ref{tab:type_mapping}.
Observations in each group are then generated from the cosinor model in
\eqref{eq:cosinor_model}, applied separately to groups $k\in\{1,2\}$ with
group-specific parameters
$(\mu_{g,k}^{(s)},A_{g,k}^{(s)},\phi_{g,k}^{(s)},\sigma_{g,k}^{(s)})$
and collection times $t_{k,i}^{(s)}$.

\emph{Step 3 --- DCP inference.}
Given a simulated dataset $\{y_{g,k,i}^{(s)}, t_{k,i}^{(s)}\}$, inference
proceeds via \textsc{DiffCircaPipeline} (DCP; \citep{xue2023diffcircapipeline})
in three sub-stages. First, for each group $k$ and gene $g$, the linearized
cosinor model is fit by OLS and an $F$-test $p$-value $p_{g,k}$ is computed.
Second, each gene is assigned a Type of Joint Rhythmicity (TOJR) label using
unadjusted $p$-values:
\begin{equation}
\hat{Z}_g =
\begin{cases}
\texttt{arrhy}   & p_{g,1}>\alpha \text{ and } p_{g,2}>\alpha,\\
\texttt{g1-only} & p_{g,1}\leq\alpha \text{ and } p_{g,2}>\alpha,\\
\texttt{g2-only} & p_{g,1}>\alpha \text{ and } p_{g,2}\leq\alpha,\\
\texttt{both}    & p_{g,1}\leq\alpha \text{ and } p_{g,2}\leq\alpha.
\end{cases}
\label{eq:tojr}
\end{equation}
Unadjusted $p$-values are used because TOJR assigns conditional gene pools
for downstream testing rather than making final discovery claims; BH correction
is reserved for the endpoint-specific tests. Third, endpoint-specific tests are
applied within the data-derived strata. For DR, all genes with
$\hat{Z}_g\neq\texttt{arrhy}$ are tested for $H_0: R_{g,1}^2 = R_{g,2}^2$,
where $R_{g,k}^2 = 1 - \mathrm{RSS}_{g,k}/\mathrm{TSS}_{g,k}$, via a
$\Delta R^2$ likelihood ratio test \citep{ding2021likelihood} followed by BH
correction. In the simulator, DR truth is defined through gain or loss of
rhythmicity at the parameter level, whereas detection follows the
\textsc{DiffCircaPipeline} test statistic. For DP, genes with $\hat{Z}_g=\texttt{both}$ are tested jointly
for $H_0: A_{g,1}=A_{g,2},\,\phi_{g,1}=\phi_{g,2}$, with a post-hoc phase
test applying \v{S}id\'{a}k correction and BH applied within the jointly
rhythmic stratum. For DM, all genes are tested for $H_0:\mu_{g,1}=\mu_{g,2}$
via a two-sample $t$-test on OLS-estimated mesors $\hat{\mu}_{g,k}$, followed
by BH correction across all genes; DM detection is TOJR-independent since a
mesor shift can occur regardless of rhythmicity status.

\emph{Step 4 --- Power and FDR summarization.}
For each endpoint $E\in\{\mathrm{DR},\mathrm{DP},\mathrm{DM}\}$ and simulation
replicate $s$, let $R_s^E=\sum_g D_{g,s}^E$ denote the number of discoveries
and $V_s^E=\sum_g D_{g,s}^E(1-Z_{g,s}^E)$ the number of false discoveries. The
per-replicate FDR is
$\widehat{\mathrm{FDR}}_s^E = V_s^E/R_s^E$ when $R_s^E>0$, and 0 otherwise.
To evaluate detection of biologically meaningful effects, let
$Z_{g,s}^{*,E}$ indicate genes that are both truly differential for endpoint
$E$ and exceed the user-specified effect-size threshold $\Delta_E$. The
per-replicate targeted power is then the proportion of these above-threshold
true positives that are detected:
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{TPower}}_s^E(\Delta_E)
=
\frac{\sum_g D_{g,s}^E Z_{g,s}^{*,E}}{\sum_g Z_{g,s}^{*,E}}.
\label{eq:tpower_def}
\end{equation}
Design-level power and FDR summaries are obtained by averaging the
corresponding per-replicate quantities across the $S$ simulation replicates.
Genes may additionally be stratified by $r_g = A_g/\sigma_g$, phase $\phi_g$,
or design efficiency $d(\phi_g)$: stratum-specific targeted power is computed
as the fraction of truly differential, above-threshold genes in stratum $j$
that are discovered, revealing which classes of genes are detectable under a
given design.

\subsection{Design Considerations: Time Coverage vs.\ Replication Trade-off}
\label{sec:design_bvsm}

In active designs, let $B$ denote the number of distinct sampling time points
and $m$ the number of replicates per time point, so that the total sample size
is $n = Bm$ for a balanced design. Investigators must therefore allocate a
fixed subject budget between temporal coverage ($B$) and replication ($m$).
Under the idealized cosinor model for a balanced
equally spaced design with $B\geq 3$ time points, the design factor satisfies
$d(\phi)=1/2$ for all gene phases $\phi$
(Supplementary~S8). Consequently, the noncentrality parameter
\eqref{eq:ncp} reduces to $\lambda_g = r_g^2 n / 2$, which depends on total
$n$ but not on the $B$-vs-$m$ allocation. This is the $B$-invariance property:
\emph{for single-harmonic cosinor inference on a balanced equally spaced grid,
resampling effort between time points and replicates does not affect power.}

In practice, this idealized result can break down. The full \textsc{SCP}
pipeline includes rhythmicity screening, DR/DP/DM testing, and BH multiplicity
correction, all of which interact with the design in non-trivial ways. At
low signal-to-noise ratios, denser time sampling ($\uparrow B$, $\downarrow m$)
can improve rhythmicity screening power even at fixed $n$, because the cosinor
model benefits from broader phase coverage. Conversely, designs with very few
time points ($B=3$) are susceptible to second-harmonic aliasing artifacts
(Supplementary~S7).

\textsc{SCP} evaluates the B-vs-$m$ trade-off directly by sweeping both $B$
and $n$ in the simulation grid, producing joint power surfaces that reveal
whether the allocation matters for a given pilot dataset and target endpoint.

\subsection{Sensitivity Analysis via Bootstrap}
\label{sec:bootstrap}

The workflow described above constitutes a \emph{two-stage plug-in} procedure:
pilot parameters are estimated once from the data, and the simulation
grid is then evaluated treating those estimates as fixed. For
endpoint $E$, the resulting power curve is
$\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}_{\mathrm{TS}}^{E}(n)
= S^{-1}\sum_{s=1}^{S}\widehat{\mathrm{TPower}}_s^{E}(n)$, and the minimum
recommended sample size is
$\hat{n}_{80}^{E} = \min\{n: \widehat{\mathrm{Power}}_{\mathrm{TS}}^{E}(n)\geq 0.80\}$.
This plug-in estimate is computationally efficient but treats pilot uncertainty
as zero, which can lead to over-optimistic projections when the pilot is small
($n_0 < 20$) or the signal is weak ($\tilde{r} < 0.5$).

To quantify pilot uncertainty, \textsc{SCP} augments the plug-in analysis
with a nonparametric bootstrap over the fitted pilot parameter table. In each
bootstrap replicate $b=1,\ldots,R_{\mathrm{boot}}$, a resample of $G_0$
gene-level rows
$\hat{\Theta}_g=(\hat{\mu}_g,\hat{A}_g,\hat{\sigma}_g,\hat{\phi}_g,\hat{Z}_g^R)$
is drawn with replacement to form $\widehat{\Theta}^{(b)}$; bootstrap
empirical distributions
$(\hat{F}_\mu^{(b)}, \hat{F}_{A,\sigma}^{(b)}, \hat{F}_\phi^{(b)})$
are reconstructed from this resample; and the data generation and inference
pipeline is run over the design grid using bootstrap inputs, averaging over
$S_{\mathrm{inner}}$ inner simulation replicates to obtain
$\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}^{E,(b)}(n,B)
= S_{\mathrm{inner}}^{-1}\sum_s \widehat{\mathrm{TPower}}_s^{E,(b)}(n,B)$.
Because the resampling unit is the entire fitted row $\hat{\Theta}_g$, the
bootstrap preserves the empirical joint distribution of $(A,\sigma)$, from
which all other simulator inputs are derived. The collection-time distribution
$\hat{F}_{\mathrm{TOD}}$ is held fixed, so the bootstrap quantifies gene-level
parameter uncertainty conditional on the observed sampling schedule.

The bootstrap mean power $\bar{P}^{E}(n,B)$ is the average of
$\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}^{E,(b)}(n,B)$ across $b=1,\ldots,R_{\mathrm{boot}}$
replicates, and the pointwise $(1-\alpha_{\mathrm{CI}})$ confidence interval is
\[
\mathrm{CI}^{E}(n,B)
=
\bigl[
\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}^{E}_{\,\alpha_{\mathrm{CI}}/2}(n,B),\;
\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}^{E}_{\,1-\alpha_{\mathrm{CI}}/2}(n,B)
\bigr],
\]
where $\widehat{\mathrm{Power}}^{E}_{q}$ denotes the $q$-th empirical quantile
across bootstrap replicates. Narrow intervals indicate stable power
projections; wide intervals signal that the pilot is insufficiently
informative to support a precise sample-size recommendation, and a larger pilot
or additional assumptions are warranted.

\subsection{Active Design: Multi-Dataset Power and Effect-Size Scaling}

To evaluate \textsc{SCP} across a wider range of study designs and effect sizes, we analyzed three additional pilot datasets spanning a 5-fold range in effect size. The mouse LIV vs.\ CER comparison (GSE54651; $n_{\text{pilot}} = 8$, $B = 8$, $m = 1$; Zhang et al.\ \citep{zhang2014comprehensive}) has median signal-to-noise ratio $\tilde{r} = 2.88$ and $\hat{\pi}_{\mathrm{DR}} = 29.6\%$, representing the highest-signal scenario. This reinforces the need for effect-size-aware, dataset-specific simulation rather than a one-size-fits-all power calculation.

\subsection{Maximizing Power: Time-Point Coverage vs.\ Replication}

For active designs, total sample size $N$ is allocated across $B$ distinct collection time points with $m = N/B$ replicates per time point. Here $B$ is the number of distinct time points and $m$ is the replication per time point. Proposition~\ref{prop:b_invariance} (Section~\ref{sec:design_theory}) establishes that for a pure sinusoidal waveform, DR power depends only on $N$ (not on $B$), implying that B and m are interchangeable given fixed $N$. We evaluate this proposition empirically using bootstrap power grids over $(N, B)$ for the baboon and D1D2 datasets.

For the baboon dataset ($\tilde{r} = 1.72$), B-invariance holds approximately: DR power at $B = 6$ and $B = 12$ are within 1\,pp at all $N$ (e.g., $N = 60$: $B = 6$ gives 79.6\% [78.7\%, 80.3\%] vs.\ $B = 12$ gives 80.3\% [79.5\%, 80.9\%]). Both reach 80\% power at $N = 60$. Reducing to $B = 4$ incurs a 7--9\,pp penalty (73.6\% [72.8\%, 74.3\%] at $N = 60$), suggesting that $B = 4$ is insufficient to resolve the 24-h sinusoid precisely.

For the mouse D1D2 dataset ($\tilde{r} = 0.65$), the B effect is more pronounced: $B = 6$ gives more than twice the DR power of $B = 3$ at all tested $N$ (e.g., $N = 150$: $B = 6$ gives 42.8\% vs.\ $B = 3$ gives 18.3\%). This departure from B-invariance reflects the pilot data's real gene expression structure---including non-sinusoidal waveforms and temporal autocorrelation---which the pure-sinusoid simulation ($\alpha_2 = 0$) does not capture. In practice, denser temporal coverage ($B \geq 6$) improves cosinor model fit when $\tilde{r}$ is low and the sinusoidal assumption may not hold precisely.

For robustness of the cosinor assumption to non-sinusoidal waveforms, see Supplementary Section~S7. Briefly, at second-harmonic strength $\alpha_2 = 0.5$ and $N \geq 48$, DR power loss relative to the pure-sinusoid baseline is at most 2--9\,pp for $B \geq 4$, confirming that the cosinor model is adequate for the active designs considered here.

\subsection{Differential Rhythmicity Power}

DR power was evaluated with 15\% of genes showing gain/loss of rhythm. Marginal per-gene power averages over all target genes and therefore reflects the full $r$ distribution. At FDR 5\%, marginal power ranges from 1.1\% ($n = 20$) to 30.5\% ($n = 100$), with empirical FDR well controlled (0.018--0.028; Table~\ref{tab:main_power}). The low marginal power reflects the predominance of low-$r$ genes in human brain tissue (Table~\ref{tab:effect_sizes}). Stratified analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:dr_power}) shows that genes with $r > 1.25$ achieve $\geq$83\% power at $n = 60$, while genes with $r < 0.5$ remain below 5\% power even at $n = 100$. Panel E shows that true discoveries are concentrated in higher-$r$ strata, and panel F shows the empirical distribution of DR targets across $r$ strata. Sensitivity to the nominal FDR threshold is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:dr_power}B,D.

\subsection{Differential Phase Power}

DP was evaluated with 15\% phase-shifted genes ($\pm$6\,h uniform). Marginal power averages over all target genes and thus reflects the full $r$ distribution. Marginal power reaches 68.7\% at $n = 100$ (Table~\ref{tab:main_power}), substantially higher than DR because phase-shifted genes retain rhythmicity in both groups. In the extended analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:dp_power}, panel C), marginal DP power exceeds 80\% at $n = 160$, indicating that DP becomes well powered at modestly larger sample sizes. Genes with $r > 0.75$ achieve $\geq$92\% power at $n = 60$ (Figure~\ref{fig:dp_power}). Panel E shows that true discoveries concentrate in moderate-to-high $r$ bins, and panel F shows the empirical distribution of DP targets across $r$ strata. \subsection{Phase Shift Sensitivity}

Power depends strongly on the magnitude of the phase difference (Figure~\ref{fig:phase_shift_abdf}), which motivates a sensitivity analysis based on the phase shift magnitude $\Delta\phi$ (hours). Because a 12\,h shift is anti-phase and maximal, $\Delta\phi$ is naturally bounded to $[0,12]$\,h. At $n = 60$, marginal power increases from 0.2\% (0.5\,h shift) to 39.0\% (4\,h) and 52.6\% (8\,h), then saturates beyond 8 hours (8\,h/12\,h power ratio $= 0.99$). Even at maximal (12\,h anti-phase) shifts, marginal power does not exceed 71.3\% ($n = 100$). Stratified analysis shows that genes with $r > 0.75$ achieve $\geq$80\% power for 4\,h shifts at $n = 60$ (Figure~\ref{fig:phase_shift_abdf}A). Full results are in Supplementary Section~S2.

\subsection{Case Study: Comparing Detection Methods}

A key feature of \textsc{SCP}'s modular architecture is its ability to evaluate competing detection methods on identical simulated data, enabling objective comparisons of power and FDR. Methods such as \textsc{JTK\_CYCLE}, \textsc{RAIN}, and \textsc{MetaCycle} focus on rhythmicity detection and do not test individual circadian parameters. We therefore focus on \textsc{DiffCircaPipeline} (DCP) and \textsc{CircaCompare} (CC), which conduct gene-level inference on circadian parameters.

DCP and CircaCompare differ in both scope and statistical approach. DCP provides a complete differential analysis pipeline: it tests DR (gain/loss of rhythmicity) via a $\Delta R^2$ LRT, then tests DP among jointly rhythmic genes via constrained-MLE LRTs. CircaCompare tests per-parameter differences (DP and differential mesor) via Wald $t$-tests from per-gene NLS fits; it has no DR test equivalent because it lacks a $\Delta R^2$-type statistic for overall rhythm gain/loss. Thus, when DR is the primary question (e.g., identifying genes that lose rhythmicity in disease), DCP is the only available option.

For the shared test type (DP), we ran both methods on identical simulated datasets (Table~\ref{tab:method_comparison}; Figure~\ref{fig:method_comparison}). CircaCompare achieves 9--12 points higher DP power but at the cost of inflated FDR (8.7--19.6\% vs.\ nominal 5\%; Figure~\ref{fig:method_comparison}B), meaning a substantial fraction of apparent DP discoveries are false positives. DCP maintains proper FDR control across all sample sizes. For differential mesor (DM, no true effects), both methods control Type~I error ($<0.002$). Stratified comparison (Supplementary Section~S4) shows the power gap is concentrated in moderate-$r$ strata ($0.5 < r < 1.5$); at high $r$ both methods converge to near-perfect power.

The computational cost difference is also substantial: DCP processes $G = 5{,}000$ genes in ${\sim}2$\,s per simulation (vectorized via \texttt{limma}), while CircaCompare requires ${\sim}150$\,s (${\sim}0.03$\,s/gene $\times$ NLS). For a full power analysis with 50 simulations $\times$ 5 sample sizes, DCP completes in ${\sim}8$ minutes vs.\ ${\sim}10$ hours for CircaCompare.

\subsection{Validation}

Framework validity was confirmed through four checks (details in Supplementary Section~S5). Under the complete null, raw $p$-value rejection rates are near nominal $\alpha = 0.05$ for DR (0.054--0.072) and DP (0.052--0.065); after BH correction the false positive rate is 0.000 for all tests, confirming multiple-testing control. Power increases monotonically with sample size for all tests and $r$-strata. Power also increases with phase shift magnitude and saturates near 8\,h. Empirical FDR is at or below nominal for DR (0.018--0.028) and DP (0.020--0.063), with slight inflation only at $n = 20$.

\section{Discussion}
%% ====================================================================

Our simulation study yields five principal findings that are directly relevant to experimental design.

First, \textbf{DR detection is severely underpowered in human brain tissue}. Even at $n = 100$ per group, marginal DR power is only 30.5\%, driven by the low median effect size ($\tilde{r} = 0.556$) in post-mortem human brain. This is especially consequential for passive post-mortem studies, where samples are scarce, collection times are fixed, and design efficiency is reduced ($d \approx 0.24$ vs.\ $d = 0.5$ for active designs). Without simulation-based guidance, researchers risk committing years of tissue procurement to an underpowered study or missing the fact that targeted analysis of high-$r$ genes can yield reliable results with moderate sample sizes.

Second, \textbf{stratified power analysis is essential}. Marginal power averages across genes with heterogeneous effect sizes and masks dramatic variation: high-$r$ genes ($r > 1.25$) are reliably detected at $n = 60$ ($\geq$83\% DR power), while low-$r$ genes ($r < 0.5$) remain below 5\% power even at $n = 100$. This parallels PROPER's finding for RNA-seq \citep{wu2015proper}; in circadian data the disparity is especially large because the $r$ distribution spans a 50-fold range.

Third, \textbf{phase shift power saturates around 8 hours}. This reflects the geometric limit of cosine separation: for two cosines with identical $A$ and phase difference $\Delta\phi$, the squared amplitude of $y_1(t) - y_2(t)$ is proportional to $\sin^2(\pi\Delta\phi/\tau)$, which plateaus for $\Delta\phi > \tau/3 \approx 8$\,h.

Fourth, \textbf{the case study comparing DCP and CircaCompare illustrates how \textsc{SCP} can guide method selection}. The two methods occupy complementary niches. DCP is the only tool providing a DR test for overall gain/loss of rhythmicity, making it indispensable when DR is the primary scientific question. For the shared test type (DP), CircaCompare offers 9--12 percentage points higher power but at the cost of inflated DP FDR (8.7--19.6\% vs.\ nominal 5\%) and ${\sim}75\times$ longer computation time. For DP, DCP is preferred in discovery studies where FDR control is critical, while CircaCompare may be considered when the power gain outweighs the inflation risk and findings will be independently validated.

Fifth, \textbf{the two-stage plug-in method systematically overestimates projected power, and bootstrap resampling corrects this bias while quantifying pilot uncertainty}. By treating pilot parameter estimates as exact, the plug-in approach ignores the variability that would result from re-running the pilot study. On the baboon LUN pilot ($n = 12$ active samples), the plug-in recommendation was $\hat{n}_{80} = 36$, but at that sample size the bootstrap analysis shows projected power is only 76\% [74.8\%, 77.0\%]---a gap of 6\,pp that would result in an underpowered study. The bootstrap recommendation of $N = 48$ correctly accounts for this uncertainty. Crucially, tight bootstrap confidence intervals do not require a large pilot: the baboon design uses only 12 animals with $m = 1$ replicate per time point, yet CIs are $\leq 3$\,pp wide because 12 equally-spaced time points fully constrain the cosinor parameters. The key is design informativeness---an active design with full 24-h coverage---rather than pilot size per se.

These findings translate into concrete design guidance. For DR in human brain tissue: $n \geq 80$ for modest marginal per-gene power; targeted analyses of high-$r$ genes can succeed with $n = 40$--60. For DP ($\pm$6\,h shifts): $n = 60$ achieves 50\% marginal per-gene power. These recommendations are specific to human post-mortem brain, which has among the lowest circadian effect sizes of any tissue \citep{zong2023circapower} and therefore represents a conservative design baseline. For actively-designed mouse studies, where core-gene $r$ values are typically 3--10$\times$ higher ($r \approx 2$--$6$; CircaPower Table~1), substantially smaller sample sizes would suffice. Active designs also benefit from a higher design factor ($d = 0.5$ vs.\ ${\sim}0.24$ for passive), further reducing sample requirements. In all cases, we recommend reporting stratified alongside marginal per-gene power, as the latter can be misleading when $r$ is heterogeneous and can obscure actionable design guidance.


Several limitations should be noted. The cosinor model assumes
sinusoidal waveforms (power loss $\leq$9\,pp for $\alpha_2\leq 0.5$,
$B\geq 4$; Supplementary~S7); gene types are modeled as mutually exclusive
(compound DR+DP changes are not simulated); and passive-design results depend
on the pilot's time-of-day distribution. Full discussion in Supplementary~S9.

\textsc{SCP} is available as an open-source R framework. An end-to-end pipeline (\texttt{examples/run\_pipeline.R}) reproduces all results in this paper, and a validation suite (\texttt{examples/run\_validation.R}) implements the checks described in Section~3.6.
