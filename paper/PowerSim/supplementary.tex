\section{Detailed Power Results}
\label{sec:detailed_power}
%% ====================================================================

Tables~\ref{tab:dr_detail}--\ref{tab:da_detail} extend the main text Table~1 with simulation-level standard errors and average false discoveries for DR, DP, and DA. DR power SEs are modest (0.3--1.0 percentage points; Table~\ref{tab:dr_detail}), confirming that $N_{\text{sim}} = 50$ simulations provide stable estimates. \subsection{Power Monotonicity}

Power should increase monotonically with sample size for a well-behaved test. From the main text Table~1 and Supplementary Tables~\ref{tab:dr_detail}--\ref{tab:da_summary}, marginal power at FDR 5\% for $n \in \{20, 40, 60, 80, 100\}$:
\begin{itemize}
\item DR: 1.1\% $\to$ 5.4\% $\to$ 13.1\% $\to$ 22.4\% $\to$ 30.5\% (strictly monotonic: PASS)
\item DP: 13.3\% $\to$ 34.5\% $\to$ 50.1\% $\to$ 61.4\% $\to$ 68.7\% (strictly monotonic: PASS)
\item DA: 17.7\% $\to$ 42.6\% $\to$ 58.8\% $\to$ 69.0\% $\to$ 75.5\% (strictly monotonic: PASS)
\end{itemize}
This is also visible in the main text Figures~1C, 2C, and 3C (marginal power vs.\ sample size panels).

\subsection{Effect Size Ordering}

Power should increase with effect size magnitude. The phase sweep (Table~\ref{tab:phase_power}) confirms monotonic power increase from 0.5\,h through 12\,h at all sample sizes, consistent with the main text Figure~3C. Power saturates near 8\,h (8h/12h ratio $= 0.99$), reflecting the geometric limit of cosine separation.

\subsection{FDR Calibration}

Empirical FDR was computed as the average proportion of false discoveries among all BH-rejections. From Tables~\ref{tab:dr_detail}--\ref{tab:da_summary}:
\begin{itemize}
\item DR: empirical FDR ranges from 0.018 ($n = 40$) to 0.028 ($n = 20$), all below the nominal 5\%.
\item DP: empirical FDR ranges from 0.020 ($n = 80$) to 0.063 ($n = 20$). Slight inflation at $n = 20$ is expected due to few total rejections, where a single false discovery disproportionately affects the FDR estimate.
\item DA: empirical FDR ranges from 0.002 ($n = 20$) to 0.007 ($n = 100$), the most conservative of the three tests, consistent with the \v{S}id\'{a}k correction applied to the DA LRT.
\end{itemize}
Phase sweep FDR (Table~\ref{tab:phase_fdr}) further confirms proper control across a range of effect sizes.

\subsection{Stratification Necessity}

At $n = 60$, DR stratified power ranges from near 0\% (low $r$) to $>$83\% (high $r$), while marginal power is 13.1\% (main text Figure~1A). DP stratified power ranges from near 0\% to $>$92\% (main text Figure~2A). DA stratified power ranges from 1\% ($r < 0.25$) to 100\% ($r > 0.75$), while marginal power is 58.8\% (main text Figure~3A). Across all three tests, the within-stratum range exceeds 80 percentage points at $n = 60$, confirming that marginal power is a misleading summary when effect sizes are heterogeneous, justifying the stratified analysis approach advocated by PROPER \cite{wu2015proper}.

\section{Simulation Algorithm}
\label{sec:algorithm}
%% ====================================================================

The following pseudocode describes the data generation procedure for a single simulation replicate at sample size $n$, as implemented in \texttt{simCircadianDiff()}.

\begin{figure}[H]
\small
\hrule\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Algorithm S1}: \texttt{simCircadianDiff} --- Two-group circadian simulation\vspace{2pt}
\hrule\vspace{4pt}
\textbf{Input}: $G$ genes, sample size $n$, proportions $\pi_R, \pi_{\text{DR}}, \pi_{\text{DP}}, \pi_{\text{DA}}$, empirical mesor distribution $\hat{F}_M$, empirical joint amplitude-noise distribution $\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$, empirical phase distribution $\hat{F}_{\phi}$, phase shift $\Delta\phi$, amplitude fold-change range $[a_{\min}, a_{\max}]$, pilot collection times $\mathbf{t}_{\text{pilot}}$, seed $s$ \\
\textbf{Output}: Expression matrices $\mathbf{Y}_1, \mathbf{Y}_2 \in \mathbb{R}^{G \times n}$, ground truth $\mathbf{D}$\vspace{4pt}

\begin{enumerate}\setlength{\itemsep}{2pt}
\item \textbf{Set seed}$(s)$. Resample with replacement for $g = 1,\ldots,G$: mesor $M_g$ from $\hat{F}_M$, the paired amplitude-noise tuple $(A_g,\sigma_g)$ jointly from $\hat{F}_{A,\sigma}$, and phase $\phi_g$ from $\hat{F}_{\phi}$. Joint sampling of $(A_g,\sigma_g)$ preserves the pilot-estimated gene-level signal-to-noise distribution $r_g = A_g/\sigma_g$.

\item \textbf{Assign gene types sequentially} (independent of $n$; all types mutually exclusive):
  (a) Sample $\lfloor G \cdot \pi_{\text{DR}} \rfloor$ DR genes from the full pool; split equally into type~2 (G1-only rhythmic) and type~3 (G2-only rhythmic).
  (b) From the remaining non-DR genes, sample $\lfloor (G - n_{\text{DR}}) \cdot \pi_R \rfloor$ as \emph{rhythmic-in-both}.
  (c) From rhythmic-in-both, sample $\lfloor G \cdot \pi_{\text{DP}} \rfloor$ as type~4 (DP).
  (d) From rhythmic-in-both \emph{excluding DP genes}, sample $\lfloor G \cdot \pi_{\text{DA}} \rfloor$ as type~5 (DA).
  (e) Remaining rhythmic-in-both genes become type~1 (rhythmic, no difference); all other genes are type~0 (arrhythmic).
  Note: a gene cannot simultaneously be DR, DP, and DA; compound multi-parameter changes are not modeled.

\item \textbf{Set group-specific parameters}. For rhythmic genes, initialize group~1 with $(A_{g,1}, \phi_{g,1}) = (A_g, \phi_g)$. Then modify group~2 according to gene type:
  type~2: $A_{g,2} = 0$ (arrhythmic in G2);
  type~3: $A_{g,1} = 0$, draw a fresh paired tuple $(A_{g,2}, \sigma_{g,2})$ and phase $\phi_{g,2}$ from the corresponding pilot-derived empirical distributions;
  type~4: $\phi_{g,2} = (\phi_{g,1} + \Delta\phi) \bmod 24$, $A_{g,2} = A_{g,1}$;
  type~5: $A_{g,2} = A_{g,1} \cdot u$, $u \sim \text{Unif}(a_{\min}, a_{\max})$.

\item \textbf{Set seed}$(s + 7919n)$. Generate collection times $t_{k,i}$ for group $k \in \{1,2\}$ and $i = 1,\ldots,n$. For passive designs, sample from the pilot-implied empirical time-of-day distribution estimated from $\mathbf{t}_{\text{pilot}}$ using a circular density estimator. For active designs, assign times deterministically from the user-specified schedule.

\item \textbf{Generate expression}. For each gene $g$, group $k$, and sample $i$:
\[
Y_{g,k,i}(t_i) = M_g + A_{g,k}\cos(\omega t_i - \omega\phi_{g,k}) + \varepsilon_{g,k,i}, \quad \varepsilon_{g,k,i} \stackrel{\text{iid}}{\sim} N(0, \sigma_{g,k}^2), \quad \omega = 2\pi/24
\]

\item \textbf{Record ground truth}: gene type, $r_{g,k} = A_{g,k}/\sigma_{g,k}$, phase and amplitude differences.
\end{enumerate}
\vspace{2pt}\hrule
\end{figure}

The two-stage seeding strategy (steps 1 and 4) ensures that gene-type assignments and baseline biological parameters are identical across sample sizes within a simulation, while the $n$-dependent reseed in step~4 introduces appropriate stochastic variation in collection times and expression noise. In particular, the initial seed fixes all $n$-independent biological quantities---such as gene-type indicators and baseline parameters $(M_g, A_g, \sigma_g, \phi_g)$---so that the true differential structure is held constant across $n$. The reseed of the form $s + 7919n$ then produces independent realizations of sampling times $t_i$ and residual errors $\varepsilon_{gi}$ for each sample size, preventing the dataset at $n_2 > n_1$ from being a simple extension of the dataset at $n_1$. Consequently, comparisons of $\mathrm{Power}(n)$ isolate the effect of sample size on sampling variability while keeping the underlying biological configuration fixed.

\section{Amplitude Fold-Change Sensitivity: Full Results}
\label{sec:amp_sweep}
%% ====================================================================

Table~\ref{tab:amp_sweep_power} presents marginal DA power across 5 fold-change values and 5 sample sizes, corresponding to the main text Figure~4. Power is negligible for 1.25$\times$ fold-changes ($<2\%$ at all $n \leq 100$), rises sharply for 2$\times$ (4.7\%--54.2\%), and approaches the main DA scenario levels at 3$\times$--4$\times$ (compare to Table~\ref{tab:da_summary} main DA results at $[2,4]\times$). Three patterns are notable: (1) even a 2$\times$ fold-change crosses 33\% power at $n \geq 80$; (2) the 3$\times$ and 4$\times$ results are lower than the main DA scenario (Table~\ref{tab:da_summary}) because the sweep uses \emph{fixed} fold-change ($\text{amp\_diff} = (fc, fc)$) while the main scenario draws from a \emph{uniform} $[2,4]\times$ distribution, yielding higher average effect; and (3) at $n=100$, both 3$\times$ and 4$\times$ exceed 78\%, confirming that moderate-to-large amplitude differences are well powered at feasible sample sizes.

\section{Robustness of the Cosinor Model Assumption}
\label{sec:fourier_robustness}
%% ====================================================================

The cosinor model assumes that each gene's rhythmic expression follows a pure sinusoid. In practice, circadian waveforms can deviate from this ideal---some genes peak sharply, others have asymmetric shapes---and the key question is whether such deviations affect power projections. To evaluate this, we simulated data in which the true gene signal contains a controlled amount of non-sinusoidal distortion. Specifically, we added a second-harmonic component to the generating model, so the true signal follows $A[\cos(2\pi t/24) + \alpha_2 \cos(4\pi t/24 + \phi_2)]$, where $\alpha_2 \in [0, 1]$ controls the degree of departure from a pure sinusoid ($\alpha_2 = 0$ recovers the standard cosinor; $\alpha_2 = 1$ means the second harmonic is as large as the fundamental). We then ran DR power simulations at $\alpha_2 \in \{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0\}$ and third-harmonic $\alpha_3 = 0$, using 20 simulation replicates and 3,000 genes across all three active-design datasets.

Key findings (Figure~\ref{fig:fourier_robustness}):

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The cosinor assumption is confirmed to be adequate at $\alpha_2 = 0$}: across all datasets and all $B$ values tested, DR power at $\alpha_2 = 0$ differs by $\leq 1$\,pp for a given $N$. This is the empirical confirmation of Proposition~1 and validates the sinusoidal simulation baseline.
  \item \textbf{Power projections are robust to moderate non-sinusoidal distortion for $B \geq 4$}: at $\alpha_2 = 0.5$ and $N \geq 48$, DR power loss relative to $\alpha_2 = 0$ is at most 2--9\,pp across all datasets. Empirical measurements of bulk RNA-seq circadian waveforms suggest second-harmonic amplitudes rarely exceed $\alpha_2 \approx 0.5$ \cite{hughes2009harmonics}, so this range covers realistic departures from the sinusoidal assumption in practice.
  \item \textbf{Single-replicate designs ($m = 1$) are sensitive to non-sinusoidal waveforms at small $N$}: for the Mouse LIV vs.\ CER dataset ($\tilde{r} = 2.9$, $B = 12$, $m = 1$), DR power at $N = 12$ collapses from 58.6\% (at $\alpha_2 = 0$) to 0.2\% (at $\alpha_2 = 0.5$). Without within-timepoint replication, the cosinor model cannot distinguish waveform-induced residual variation from biological noise. Adding $m = 2$ replicates per timepoint substantially attenuates this sensitivity.
  \item \textbf{$B = 3$ equally-spaced timepoints is algebraically problematic and should be avoided}: at $B = 3$ equally-spaced timepoints (e.g., 0, 8, 16\,h), the second-harmonic function $\cos(4\pi t/24)$ takes identical values to $\cos(2\pi t/24)$ at the sample points. This aliasing means the cosinor model absorbs the second-harmonic signal into its amplitude estimate rather than treating it as residual noise, causing DR power to increase artifactually with $\alpha_2$. $B = 3$ should not be used for circadian sampling.
\end{enumerate}
